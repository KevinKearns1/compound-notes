I worked through [Traditional vs. Roth IRA]({{ '/traditional-vs-roth-ira/' | relative_url }}) with the actual math and ended that post by noting that employer 401(k) plans increasingly offer the same Traditional-or-Roth choice. I never came back to it. The core tax logic transfers over directly — but a 401(k) adds two differences that change the decision for some people, and one of them matters a lot more than it sounds.

## The one-sentence version

The Traditional-vs-Roth tax tradeoff inside a 401(k) is identical to the one inside an IRA (pay tax now or pay tax later), but a 401(k) has no income limit on choosing Roth, lets you split contributions between both in the same year, and — because it comes out of payroll — makes the real cost difference show up directly in your paycheck instead of a once-a-year contribution decision.

<!--more-->

## The part that's exactly the same

**Traditional 401(k):** contributions reduce your taxable income this year, grow tax-deferred, and get taxed as ordinary income when you withdraw in retirement. **Roth 401(k):** contributions are made with money you've already paid tax on, grow completely tax-free, and owe nothing on withdrawal. That's the identical mechanic covered in the [IRA post]({{ '/traditional-vs-roth-ira/' | relative_url }}), including the same conclusion: if your tax rate is the same now as it'll be in retirement, the two options produce the exact same after-tax result, and the entire decision comes down to whether you expect your rate to be higher now or later.

If you haven't read that post, the worked table there is the actual math — I won't re-derive it here. What's genuinely different about the 401(k) version is everything below.

## Difference 1: there's no income limit on a Roth 401(k)

This is the one that actually changes behavior. A [Roth IRA]({{ '/what-is-a-roth-ira/' | relative_url }}) has an income cap — above a certain income, you can't contribute to one directly at all. **A Roth 401(k) has no such limit, at any income level.** This means a high earner who's completely phased out of a Roth IRA can still get Roth-style tax-free growth by using a Roth 401(k) instead, assuming their employer's plan offers one. For someone in that position, this isn't a minor technicality — it's the only direct path to a Roth account they have, and it's worth confirming whether the employer plan actually offers the Roth option before assuming it doesn't apply.

## Difference 2: the real cost shows up differently, paycheck to paycheck

An IRA contribution is usually a decision you make directly — transfer money in, once or on your own schedule. A 401(k) contribution comes out of every paycheck automatically, which makes the actual cost difference between Traditional and Roth visible in a way an IRA doesn't show as clearly. Here's $500 a month, at a 22% marginal tax rate:

| | Reduces take-home pay by | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional 401(k) | **$390/month** | Pre-tax — you don't pay income tax on this $500 this year |
| Roth 401(k) | **$500/month** | After-tax — this $500 was already taxed as ordinary income |

The $110/month difference isn't free money disappearing — it's deferred tax, and it's exactly why the two options tie at an identical future tax rate: that $110/month, if actually invested elsewhere rather than just spent, grows into precisely the gap between a Traditional account's after-tax value and a Roth account's tax-free value by retirement. The catch is in that word *if*. A Traditional 401(k)'s "advantage" at an equal tax rate only shows up for someone who reinvests the extra take-home pay — someone who spends it instead is quietly accepting a worse deal than the equal-rate math suggests, without the paycheck ever telling them so.

## Difference 3: the employer match's own tax treatment

Most 401(k) plans still deposit an [employer match]({{ '/401k-employer-match/' | relative_url }}) as pre-tax money, regardless of whether you elected Traditional or Roth for your own contributions — meaning a Roth 401(k) participant can end up with a single account holding both a tax-free Roth bucket (their contributions) and a taxable Traditional bucket (the employer match) at the same time, tracked separately inside the same plan. Some newer plans now let employees elect Roth treatment on the match itself, but that's a plan-specific option, not something to assume is available — worth checking directly with your plan rather than guessing either way.

## Difference 4: you can split, deliberately, in the same year

An IRA forces a yearly either/or choice between account types (short of having both open and deciding where each contribution goes). A 401(k) typically lets you split your own contribution between Traditional and Roth within the same plan, same year, in whatever proportion you choose — 50/50, 80/20, or any other split. This is a genuine hedge against the one thing nobody can actually know, which the IRA post already named honestly: your own future tax bracket. Splitting doesn't require guessing right. It just means you're not fully exposed if you guess wrong.

## So which one should you actually pick

The same question decides it as with an IRA: do you expect your tax rate to be higher now or in retirement? Early-career and likely to earn more later → Traditional's tax break probably isn't worth much yet, and Roth's tax-free growth has decades to compound. Near peak earnings, expecting a lower-spending retirement → Traditional's upfront deduction is doing real work at a rate you may not see again. Genuinely unsure → splitting is a reasonable default, not a cop-out, precisely because a 401(k) makes it easy to do.

The one case where the decision isn't really a toss-up: if you're a high earner locked out of a Roth IRA by the income limit and your employer's 401(k) offers a Roth option, that's not a coin flip against Traditional — it's close to the only way you get tax-free retirement growth at all, and it's worth using for that reason alone even before running the rate comparison.

## The takeaway

The tax math inside a 401(k) is the same tradeoff as an IRA, so if you've already worked through [that decision]({{ '/traditional-vs-roth-ira/' | relative_url }}), you already understand the core of this one too. What's actually new here is structural: no income limit gates the Roth option, the employer match usually arrives pre-tax no matter what you choose, and splitting between both is a standing option rather than a once-a-year pivot. None of that changes the fundamental question — it just changes who the Roth 401(k) is quietly a better deal for, and it's worth checking your own plan's specifics rather than assuming the IRA rules apply unchanged.
