I wrote about the [HSA's triple tax break]({{ '/what-is-an-hsa/' | relative_url }}) for medical costs a while back — a 529 plan is the closer parallel for education costs than most people realize, and it's similarly underused relative to how good the deal actually is.

## The one-sentence version

A 529 plan is a tax-advantaged investment account specifically for education expenses — contributions grow tax-free, and withdrawals are tax-free too, as long as the money is used for qualified education costs.

<!--more-->

## How it actually works

You (or anyone — a parent, grandparent, or the future student themselves) open a 529 account, typically for a specific beneficiary, and contribute money that gets invested, commonly in [age-based or risk-based fund options]({{ '/stocks-vs-bonds-by-age/' | relative_url }}) similar in spirit to a [target-date fund]({{ '/what-is-a-target-date-fund/' | relative_url }}) but aimed at a college start date instead of a retirement date. The money grows [tax-free]({{ '/what-is-compound-interest/' | relative_url }}), and withdrawals are tax-free too, as long as they're used for **qualified education expenses**: tuition, fees, room and board, books, and required equipment — at colleges, and in many states, K-12 tuition up to an annual limit.

The tax structure is: no federal deduction for contributions (unlike a [Traditional 401(k) or IRA]({{ '/traditional-vs-roth-ira/' | relative_url }})), but **many states offer a state income tax deduction or credit** for contributions to that state's own plan — worth checking specifically, since it varies widely and some states offer it for contributions to any state's plan, not just their own.

## Why this is a genuinely good deal, with real numbers

Say you contribute $200/month starting when a child is born, invested at a 6% average return, until they turn 18 — that's $43,200 contributed over 18 years. At 6% growth, the account would be worth roughly **$77,500** by the time they start college. Compare that to the same $43,200 sitting in a regular taxable account, where the roughly $34,300 of growth would be subject to [capital gains tax]({{ '/capital-gains-tax-explained/' | relative_url }}) when withdrawn — in a 529, that same growth comes out completely untaxed as long as it goes toward qualified expenses, which for an 18-year horizon is a substantial, real tax saving.

## The honest limits and caveats

**You're betting on using it for education.** If the beneficiary doesn't end up needing all of it for qualified expenses, non-qualified withdrawals owe income tax plus a 10% penalty on the earnings portion — the contribution portion always comes out penalty-free since it was already taxed income, but the growth doesn't get the tax-free treatment if it's not used for its intended purpose.

**You can change the beneficiary.** If one child doesn't use all the funds, the account can usually be reassigned to a sibling, another family member, or even back to a parent for their own qualifying education — a real, flexible escape valve that reduces the "what if they don't go to college" risk more than people assume.

**A limited amount can now roll into a Roth IRA.** Recent rule changes allow a capped lifetime amount (subject to annual Roth contribution limits and other conditions) of unused 529 funds to roll into a [Roth IRA]({{ '/what-is-a-roth-ira/' | relative_url }}) for the beneficiary, under specific conditions (the account must have been open a minimum number of years, among other rules) — meaningfully reducing the downside of oversaving, though it's not unlimited and the rules are specific enough to be worth checking directly rather than assuming.

**Financial aid impact is real but often overstated.** A 529 owned by a parent is counted as a parental asset on federal financial aid calculations, which has a smaller impact on aid eligibility than people often assume — generally a much smaller effect than, say, income, and smaller than if the same money were in the student's own name instead.

## Why this is similar to, but not the same as, an HSA

Both are accounts built around a single qualifying purpose, both offer real [tax-free growth]({{ '/what-is-compound-interest/' | relative_url }}), and both reward starting early for exactly the compounding reasons covered [elsewhere on this blog]({{ '/what-is-compound-interest/' | relative_url }}). The difference: an HSA's "not used for its purpose" penalty disappears entirely after 65 (it just becomes a regular [retirement account]({{ '/what-is-a-brokerage-account/' | relative_url }}) at that point), while a 529's penalty for non-qualified use doesn't have that same automatic off-ramp — the Roth IRA rollover option is the closest equivalent, but it's capped and conditional rather than unconditional.

## Who this actually makes sense for

Anyone with reasonable confidence that a child (or themselves, or another family member) will have real education expenses eventually — the beneficiary-change flexibility makes "which specific person" less important than "is education likely to happen somewhere in the family." It's not the right place for money you might need for something else, given the penalty on non-qualified use, and it's not a replacement for retirement savings — the same way an HSA shouldn't replace a 401(k) either, even though both offer real tax advantages worth using for their intended purpose.

## The takeaway

A 529 plan is a real, often underused tax break specifically for education costs, and the earlier it starts, the more of its value comes from decades of tax-free compounding rather than the contributions themselves. The main things worth actually checking before opening one: your state's specific deduction rules, and being honest with yourself about how likely the money is to actually go toward qualified education expenses for someone in the family.
