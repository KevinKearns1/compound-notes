I've covered what moves a [credit score]({{ '/what-actually-moves-your-credit-score/' | relative_url }}) and how to [build credit from scratch]({{ '/how-to-build-credit-from-scratch/' | relative_url }}) without ever covering the free federal protection most people skip entirely — usually because they assume it costs money, hurts their score, or is the same thing as a different product with a similar name.

## The one-sentence version

A credit freeze blocks new credit from being opened at a specific credit bureau until you lift it, a fraud alert requires lenders to verify your identity before extending credit without blocking anything, and a credit lock is a different, bureau-branded commercial product that isn't bound by the same federal protections as a freeze — and all three get confused with each other constantly.

<!--more-->

## The credit freeze: free, and it doesn't touch your score

A federal law made credit freezes free at all three major credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) starting in 2018 — no cost to place one, lift it temporarily, or remove it permanently, ever. A freeze blocks that bureau from releasing your credit report to a new lender, which in practice means a new credit application in your name can't be approved while it's active, because the lender can't pull the report needed to approve it. **It does not affect your credit score** — this is one of the most common misconceptions about it, likely from confusing "freezing your credit" with something that sounds like it would freeze the number itself. It doesn't. A frozen credit file still accrues payment history and utilization normally; it just can't be used to open something new.

## The trap: you have to do this at all three bureaus, separately

This is the detail that catches people off guard, specifically because it's the *opposite* of how the other tool below works. A freeze at Equifax does nothing to protect against an application pulled from Experian or TransUnion — **each bureau's freeze has to be placed individually**, since any one of the three could be used by a lender to approve a new account. Someone who freezes one bureau and assumes they're covered is only a third of the way protected.

## The fraud alert: one call covers all three

A fraud alert works the other direction. Placing one at **any single bureau** requires that bureau, by law, to notify the other two — so one fraud alert covers all three without needing to repeat the process three times. It doesn't block anything the way a freeze does; it just requires a lender to take extra steps to verify your identity before approving new credit in your name, which slows down a fraudulent application without preventing a legitimate one from going through with some extra verification.

A standard fraud alert lasts **one year**; an **extended fraud alert**, available specifically to confirmed identity theft victims with a police report or FTC identity theft report on file, lasts **seven years**.

## The credit lock: convenient, but not the same legal protection

A credit lock is a feature offered directly by the bureaus (often bundled into a paid credit-monitoring product) that works similarly to a freeze — toggle it on or off, usually instantly via an app — but it is **not** governed by the same federal statute that makes freezes free and legally mandated. Consumer protection regulators have specifically flagged this distinction: a lock is a commercial convenience feature with terms set by whichever company offers it, not a legally guaranteed protection in the same way a freeze is. Worth knowing the difference before assuming a "lock" marketed by a monitoring service carries the same guarantees as an actual freeze.

## Which one to actually use

**A credit freeze, for almost everyone, as a default.** There's no real downside — it's free, doesn't affect your score, and only costs a few minutes of hassle to lift temporarily the next time you actually need to apply for credit (a new card, a loan, an apartment credit check). For most people not actively applying for new credit on a regular basis, this is close to pure upside.

**A fraud alert, when there's a specific but non-confirmed reason for concern** — a data breach notification naming your information, for instance — especially if freezing all three bureaus feels like more hassle than warranted for a risk that hasn't been confirmed yet.

**An extended fraud alert, after confirmed identity theft**, specifically because it lasts seven years instead of one and is designed for exactly that situation.

## The takeaway

These three tools get lumped together constantly, and the confusion is exactly backwards in one important way: the free, legally mandated freeze requires action at all three bureaus separately, while the lighter-touch fraud alert needs only one. Knowing which is which, and that the freeze costs nothing and doesn't touch your score, removes the two most common reasons people skip the one protection here that's genuinely free with essentially no downside.
