I mentioned [SIPC protection]({{ '/what-is-a-brokerage-account/' | relative_url }}) as the brokerage-account equivalent of FDIC insurance without ever explaining the one way it isn't equivalent at all — and it's the exact gap that matters most.

## The one-sentence version

SIPC protects against a brokerage firm failing or mishandling customer assets — up to $500,000 per customer per separate account, including a $250,000 sub-limit specifically for cash — and it does not, under any circumstance, protect against an investment simply losing value because the market went down.

<!--more-->

## The misconception this entire topic exists to correct

Hearing "insurance" attached to a brokerage account understandably makes it sound like protection against losing money on an investment. That's not what SIPC does, and it was never designed to. SIPC exists to protect customers if the **brokerage itself** fails, commits fraud, or can't account for customer assets — the parallel to FDIC protecting a bank account if the **bank** fails, not if the money in it buys less than it used to. If a stock in a SIPC-protected account drops 50% because the market dropped, SIPC does nothing, because nothing covered by SIPC actually happened — the brokerage is still functioning normally, the loss is just the ordinary risk of owning an investment.

## What the coverage actually is

**$500,000 per customer, per separate account capacity** — with a **$250,000 sub-limit specifically for cash** that counts toward the $500,000 total, not on top of it. Someone holding $500,000 in securities plus $250,000 in cash at the same brokerage, in the same account capacity, has more total value than the coverage actually protects — the combined limit doesn't stretch to cover both amounts in full.

## The "separate capacity" detail worth knowing

Coverage applies per distinct account capacity, not simply per brokerage. A taxable brokerage account and a [Roth IRA]({{ '/what-is-a-roth-ira/' | relative_url }}) held at the same firm are different capacities and each get their own separate $500,000 limit. Two IRAs of the identical type at the same firm, on the other hand, share a single $500,000 limit between them rather than getting one each. This matters specifically for someone with substantial balances spread across multiple account types at one brokerage — the actual protected total depends on how those accounts are categorized, not just the sum of everything held there.

## The direct comparison to FDIC

| | FDIC | SIPC |
|---|---|---|
| Protects against | Bank failure | Brokerage failure or misconduct |
| Covers investment losses? | N/A — deposit accounts don't carry market risk | **No — explicitly excluded** |
| Typical limit | $250,000 per depositor, per bank | $500,000 per customer, per account capacity ($250,000 cash sub-limit) |

Both protections share the same basic shape — insuring against the institution itself failing — applied to two different kinds of accounts carrying two different kinds of risk. The crucial asymmetry: a [high-yield savings account]({{ '/high-yield-savings-accounts/' | relative_url }}) covered by FDIC doesn't have market risk to begin with, so FDIC's silence on "investment losses" isn't a gap — there's nothing of that kind to cover. A brokerage account absolutely does carry market risk, and SIPC's explicit exclusion of it is a real, meaningful gap between what the word "protected" might imply and what it actually means here.

## Why this matters practically

SIPC coverage is not a reason to take on more investment risk than otherwise makes sense — it was never insuring against that risk in the first place, so it changes nothing about how much market exposure is appropriate for any given situation. Where it becomes a genuine practical consideration is at very large account balances: someone with meaningfully more than $500,000 at a single brokerage, in a single account capacity, has an amount that exceeds what SIPC would fully protect if that specific, rare event (brokerage failure or malfeasance) actually happened — a real, if niche, reason some investors spread very large balances across more than one brokerage.

## The takeaway

SIPC is real protection, for a real but narrow risk: the brokerage itself failing or mishandling assets, not the ordinary ups and downs of whatever is actually invested. The $500,000 limit (with its $250,000 cash sub-limit) matters mainly at large account balances, and the "separate capacity" rule is worth understanding if assets are spread across multiple account types at one firm — but for the much more common worry, "what if my investments lose value," SIPC was never the answer, and no insurance product covers that, because that's simply what investing risk is.
