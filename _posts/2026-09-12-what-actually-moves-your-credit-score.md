Everything else on this site is about investing, but your credit score affects things investing never touches — the interest rate on a car loan, whether you get approved for an apartment, sometimes even job applications. I've never actually broken down what moves it, so here it is.

## What a credit score actually measures

A credit score (most commonly a FICO score, ranging 300-850) is a prediction of how likely you are to pay back borrowed money on time, based on your credit history. It's not a measure of income, savings, or net worth — you can have a great credit score with a modest income and a mediocre one with a high income, because it's specifically about borrowing and repayment behavior, not overall financial health.

## The five factors, and how much each one actually counts

Credit scoring models don't treat every factor equally — here's the general FICO breakdown:

| Factor | Roughly how much it counts | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Payment history | ~35% | Have you paid on time, or missed/late payments |
| Amounts owed | ~30% | How much of your available credit you're using |
| Length of credit history | ~15% | How long your accounts have existed |
| Credit mix | ~10% | Variety of credit types (cards, loans, etc.) |
| New credit | ~10% | Recent applications/new accounts |

The big takeaway from this table: **two factors (payment history and amounts owed) make up almost two-thirds of the score.** Everything else matters, but it matters a lot less — which means the advice that actually moves your number fastest concentrates on those two.

## Payment history: the single biggest lever

This is exactly what it sounds like: pay at least the minimum on every credit account, every time, by the due date. A single missed payment reported to the credit bureaus (usually after 30 days late) can meaningfully hurt a score, and the damage lingers — late payments can stay on a credit report for up to seven years, though the impact fades well before then. There's no clever optimization here beyond the obvious: autopay for at least the minimum payment on everything, so a forgotten due date never becomes a real problem.

## Amounts owed: the part people misunderstand

This factor is mostly about **credit utilization** — the percentage of your available credit you're actually using, not the raw dollar amount. Someone using $2,000 of a $2,500 limit (80% utilization) looks riskier to a scoring model than someone using $2,000 of a $20,000 limit (10% utilization), even though the second person has more debt in absolute dollars.

The commonly cited target is keeping utilization under 30%, and lower is generally better still. Two practical levers: pay down balances, or — less obviously — **keep old credit cards open even if you don't use them much**, since closing a card reduces your total available credit, which can push your utilization percentage up even if your spending hasn't changed at all.

## Length of history: the one that just takes time

There's no shortcut here — this factor rewards accounts that have existed for a while, which is part of why financial advice often says "don't close your oldest credit card." Closing your longest-held account can shorten your average account age, working against this factor for no real benefit if the card has no annual fee.

## Credit mix and new credit: real, but minor

Having a mix of credit types (a credit card and a car loan, say, rather than only credit cards) contributes a little, but it's not worth manufacturing — taking out a loan you don't need purely to diversify your "mix" costs you real money in interest for a small, uncertain scoring benefit. Similarly, applying for several new credit accounts in a short window causes multiple "hard inquiries," each a small, temporary dip — worth avoiding right before something like a mortgage application, not worth obsessing over otherwise.

## Myths worth actually dropping

**"Carrying a balance and paying interest helps your score."** No — this is one of the most common and costliest myths in personal finance. The scoring model only sees your reported balance and utilization, not whether you paid it off in full or carried it with interest. Paying your statement balance in full every month gives you the same utilization benefit as carrying a balance, minus the interest charge. There's no scoring upside to paying interest, ever.

**"Checking your own credit score hurts it."** No — checking your own score is a "soft inquiry" and doesn't affect it at all. Only "hard inquiries" from actually applying for new credit have a small impact. Check your own score as often as you want.

**"You need to be in debt to build credit."** No — using a credit card for normal spending and paying it off in full every month builds credit history and payment history without ever paying interest or "being in debt" in any meaningful sense.

## The takeaway

If you only do two things: pay everything on time, every time, and keep your credit utilization low (pay down balances, keep old cards open, don't max anything out). That's roughly two-thirds of the score, and it's also just... good financial behavior on its own, independent of the number it produces.
