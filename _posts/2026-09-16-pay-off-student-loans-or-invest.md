I've said "pay off high-interest debt before investing" in a few posts on here, and left student loans out of the specifics on purpose — they deserve their own answer. A credit card at 24% interest isn't a real decision (pay it off, always). A student loan at 5-6% is a genuine judgment call, and I want to actually walk through why instead of just asserting one side.

## Why this isn't the same question as credit card debt

Paying off a debt is a **guaranteed return** equal to its interest rate — pay off a 24% card, and that's a locked-in 24% return with zero risk, which no investment reliably matches. That math makes credit card debt an easy call.

Federal student loans typically sit in the 4-7% range (varies by loan type and origination year). The stock market's long-run historical average return is commonly cited around 7-10% annually — but unlike the guaranteed return from paying off debt, that number is an *average over decades*, with real years of losses mixed in. Comparing a guaranteed 5% against a not-guaranteed "maybe 8%" isn't comparing two guaranteed numbers, so the "just pick the bigger number" logic that works for credit cards doesn't cleanly apply here.

<!--more-->

## The case for paying off the loan first

**Certainty has real value.** A paid-off loan is money you'll never owe again, full stop. Market returns are variable — a 10-year stretch of below-average returns is a real possibility, not a hypothetical, and a large loan balance sitting alongside disappointing market performance can feel very different than it looked on a spreadsheet.

**Psychological relief is a legitimate factor, not a weakness.** Some people make measurably better financial decisions overall once a debt is gone — less financial stress, clearer monthly cash flow, more willingness to actually invest once nothing is hanging over it. That's not "bad math," it's accounting for a real behavioral effect spreadsheets don't capture.

**It reduces required monthly obligations.** A paid-off loan lowers your fixed monthly costs permanently, which increases your flexibility if income ever drops — closer to the [emergency fund]({{ '/emergency-fund-how-much/' | relative_url }}) logic than a pure return-on-investment question.

## The case for investing instead

**Time in the market is the thing you can't get back.** Money invested at 22 has decades to compound that money invested at 32 doesn't get back, a point I've made elsewhere on here about [starting early]({{ '/college-students-should-invest/' | relative_url }}) — paying off a loan aggressively for 5 extra years while skipping investing has a real opportunity cost that's easy to underweight because it's invisible, not because it's small.

**You're probably leaving employer money on the table if you go all-in on loans.** If your employer offers a [401(k) match]({{ '/401k-employer-match/' | relative_url }}) and you're not contributing enough to get it while aggressively prepaying a 5% loan, that's very likely the wrong trade — the match is a 50-100% instant return that beats prepaying a 5% loan under nearly any reasonable analysis.

**The expected-value math often favors investing.** Over long time horizons, the stock market's average historical return has usually exceeded typical federal student loan rates — "often" and "on average," not "guaranteed" or "every specific decade," which is exactly the uncertainty that makes this a real decision rather than an obvious one.

## A reasonable way to actually split it

This doesn't have to be all-or-nothing:

1. Pay at least the minimum on every loan, always — never skip this to invest more.
2. Get the full employer 401(k) match first, before extra loan payments — that comparison isn't close.
3. Beyond that, the split between "extra loan payments" and "investing" can reasonably scale with the loan's interest rate: a 3% loan leans hard toward investing; a 7-8% loan leans toward paying it down faster; something in between is genuinely a coin flip where either choice is defensible.
4. If the psychological weight of the debt is genuinely affecting your decisions or stress levels, that's a legitimate reason to weight toward payoff even if the pure math leans the other way — this is a real, personal decision, not a spreadsheet exercise with one correct answer.

## The takeaway

Student loan debt isn't credit card debt wearing a different name — the interest rate usually isn't high enough to make payoff the obviously correct move the way it is for high-interest cards, which means this is one of the few places in personal finance where reasonable people, doing the actual math, land in different places. Get the minimum payments and the employer match handled first; beyond that, the right split depends on your specific rate and how much the balance actually weighs on you, not a universal rule.
