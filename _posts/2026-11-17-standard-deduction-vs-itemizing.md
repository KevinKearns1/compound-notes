I mentioned that [the mortgage interest deduction mostly doesn't apply anymore]({{ '/extra-mortgage-payments-or-invest/' | relative_url }}) "for people who itemize" without ever explaining what itemizing actually is, or why so many filers don't need to think about this choice at all anymore.

## The one-sentence version

Every tax filer automatically gets a standard deduction — a flat amount subtracted from taxable income with no paperwork required — or they can itemize instead, adding up specific allowed expenses (state and local taxes up to a cap, mortgage interest, charitable donations, and a few others) and using that total if it's larger; the standard deduction has grown large enough in recent years that most filers come out ahead taking it without ever calculating the alternative.

<!--more-->

## What itemizing actually involves

Itemizing means totaling specific categories of expenses the tax code allows as deductions, reported on a separate form, instead of taking the flat standard amount. The main categories: **state and local taxes (SALT)** — income or property taxes paid, subject to a cap that has itself been a frequent target of tax-law changes in recent years (worth checking the current-year figure directly rather than assuming last year's number still applies); **mortgage interest** on a home loan; **charitable donations**; and **medical expenses** above a percentage-of-income threshold. Add up everything that qualifies, and if the total exceeds the standard deduction, itemizing saves more on taxes. If it doesn't, the standard deduction wins by default.

## Why most people never need to run this calculation

The standard deduction is large enough today that itemized totals frequently fall short of it unless specific circumstances apply — a sizable mortgage, a high-tax state, or an unusually large charitable or medical expense in a given year. Estimates commonly put the share of filers who itemize somewhere in the 10-15% range, meaning the large majority take the standard deduction without ever needing receipts, a specific form, or a reason beyond "it's already the better number." This is worth knowing explicitly: itemizing isn't a more sophisticated or more correct way to file taxes, it's simply the better number in a minority of specific situations.

## The real technique worth knowing: bunching

For someone close to the line — itemized expenses that land near, but just under, the standard deduction most years — there's a legitimate, commonly used strategy called **bunching**: concentrate two or more years' worth of charitable giving (or other flexible deductible expenses) into a single tax year, clearing the itemizing threshold that year by a meaningful margin, then take the standard deduction in the following "off" years when there's nothing to bunch. Done over a multi-year cycle, this can produce a larger total deduction across the years than taking the standard deduction every single year, without actually giving any more money overall — just timing the same giving differently.

## The honest caveat this post needs

The specific dollar figures — the standard deduction amount, the SALT cap, and other itemizing thresholds — change by tax year and have been subject to genuinely significant legislative changes recently, including caps that have moved considerably from where they sat just a few years ago. Rather than quote numbers here that may already be outdated by the time this is read, the mechanism above is durable; the actual current-year figures are not, and are worth checking directly against the current IRS guidance (or a tax preparer) rather than relying on any blog post, including this one, for the exact amount that applies to a specific filing year.

## The takeaway

Itemizing isn't a decision most filers need to agonize over — it's a comparison with a usually-obvious answer, and for the large majority of people, the standard deduction already wins without any extra work. The real value in understanding the mechanism is knowing when to check: a new mortgage, a move to a higher-tax state, or a year with an unusually large charitable or medical expense are the moments worth actually running the comparison, rather than assuming either answer applies to every year by default.
