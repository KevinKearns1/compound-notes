I've told you to buy "a broad index fund" more times than I can count on this blog, and I've never once clarified that "index fund" isn't actually a type of account or a legal structure — it's a strategy. That strategy gets sold to you wrapped in one of two very different packages: a mutual fund or an ETF. Worth actually untangling, especially before you're staring at a brokerage app trying to decide between two tickers that seem to do the same thing.

## The one-sentence version

"Index fund" describes *what* a fund does (track a market index like the S&P 500); "mutual fund" and "ETF" describe *how* it's packaged and traded — and a huge number of index funds exist in both forms, holding nearly identical investments, with real differences in how you actually buy and sell them.

<!--more-->

## What actually separates a mutual fund from an ETF

Both can hold the exact same underlying investments — hundreds or thousands of [stocks]({{ '/what-is-a-stock/' | relative_url }}), tracking the same index. The difference is entirely in the mechanics of ownership and trading:

- **Mutual funds** price and trade **once per day**, after the market closes. Submit an order any time during the day, and it executes at that day's single closing price ("net asset value"), determined after trading ends — you don't know your exact execution price the moment you place the order.
- **ETFs (exchange-traded funds)** trade **continuously throughout the day**, exactly like an individual stock. You can watch the price move minute to minute, place a limit order at a specific price, or buy and sell multiple times in the same day if you wanted to.

This single difference explains most of the other practical distinctions people notice.

## Why this practical difference matters

**Minimum investment:** many mutual funds have a minimum initial investment (often $1,000-$3,000, sometimes waived), because you're buying directly from the fund company. ETFs trade like stocks, so you can typically buy as little as one share — and many brokerages, Robinhood included, now support buying **fractional shares** of ETFs, which lowers the practical entry point to whatever dollar amount you want to start with.

**Cost:** both can have low [expense ratios]({{ '/what-is-an-expense-ratio/' | relative_url }}) for a comparable index — this isn't really a mutual-fund-vs-ETF distinction, it's a "which specific fund" distinction. But ETFs are more commonly commission-free to trade at most brokerages today, whereas some mutual funds (especially ones outside your brokerage's own fund family) can carry a transaction fee.

**Tax efficiency:** ETFs have a structural mechanism (how new shares get created and redeemed, using an in-kind process rather than cash) that tends to generate fewer taxable capital gains distributions than a comparable mutual fund, in a regular taxable brokerage account specifically. It's a real, well-documented difference, though a relatively minor one for a typical long-term, buy-and-hold investor — it matters more the more actively a fund trades internally.

## What doesn't actually differ

This is the part worth remembering when you're comparing two options that track the same index: **an S&P 500 index mutual fund and an S&P 500 index ETF are, underneath the wrapper, holding almost the same 500 companies in almost the same proportions.** The difference isn't in what you own — it's in how you buy it, how quickly you can trade it, and some secondary cost and tax mechanics. Picking between them isn't picking between fundamentally different investments; it's picking between two ways to access essentially the same one.

## When the difference actually matters for you

- **If you're investing a fixed amount on a schedule and not planning to trade intraday** (most long-term investors, most of the time), the mutual-fund-vs-ETF choice is mostly a wash — pick whichever is available at a low cost through your brokerage without a transaction fee.
- **If you want the flexibility to buy or sell at a specific price during the trading day**, or you're starting with a small dollar amount and want fractional shares, ETFs are generally the more accessible and flexible option today.
- **If your 401(k) or workplace plan only offers mutual fund options** (common — employer plans often don't offer ETFs at all), that's simply the wrapper available to you there, and the underlying index-tracking strategy still works the same way.

## The takeaway

"Index fund" tells you the strategy; "mutual fund" or "ETF" tells you the packaging. Once you know that, a ticker like VOO or VTI stops being a mystery — it's very likely the same basic idea as an S&P 500 or total-market index mutual fund, just in the form that trades like a stock, with fractional shares and same-day pricing as the practical upside. Neither wrapper is a trap; the real decision, as always, is still mostly about the [expense ratio]({{ '/what-is-an-expense-ratio/' | relative_url }}) and how broad the underlying index actually is.
