I named this entire blog after this concept and have used the word "compound" or "compounds" in nearly every post without ever stopping to explain, mechanically, what it actually means. Overdue to fix.

## The one-sentence version

Compound interest is interest earned on your interest, not just on your original amount — so your money grows faster every year, because each year it's growing on top of a bigger pile than the year before.

<!--more-->

## Simple interest vs. compound interest, side by side

**Simple interest** only ever applies to your original amount. Put $1,000 in at 7% simple interest for 30 years, and you earn $70 every single year, flat, for 30 years: $1,000 + ($70 × 30) = **$3,100**.

**Compound interest** applies to your original amount *plus* everything it's already earned. Same $1,000 at 7%, compounded annually for 30 years: **$7,612**. Same starting amount, same rate, same 30 years — more than double the result, because starting in year two, you're earning 7% on $1,070, not $1,000. By year 30, you're earning 7% on a balance that's grown more than sevenfold.

That gap between $3,100 and $7,612 is the entire concept. Nothing else about compounding is more complicated than that — it just keeps applying, year after year, to a growing number instead of a fixed one.

## Why "time in the market" isn't just a slogan

Here's the number that actually makes this concrete, and it's the one worth remembering: **two people, same monthly amount, same 7% average return — the one who starts earlier and stops sooner can end up ahead of the one who invests for three times as long.**

- **Investor A** puts $200/month into an index fund from age 25 to 35 — ten years, $24,000 total contributed — then stops adding money entirely and just leaves it invested, untouched, until age 65.
- **Investor B** waits until 35 to start, then puts in $200/month every month from 35 to 65 — thirty years, $72,000 total contributed, three times what Investor A put in.

At age 65, at a 7% average annual return:

| | Total contributed | Balance at 65 |
|---|---|---|
| Investor A (invests 25–35, then stops) | $24,000 | **~$280,968** |
| Investor B (invests 35–65) | $72,000 | **~$243,994** |

Investor A contributed a third as much money and ended up with **more**. The only difference between them is *when* the money went in — Investor A's contributions had an extra decade to compound before Investor B had contributed a single dollar, and that decade of head start was worth more than the twenty additional years of contributions Investor B made trying to catch up.

This is the actual, numerical reason "start now, even small" gets repeated so often in this kind of writing. It's not a mood — it's what the math does whenever the growth rate is meaningfully larger than zero and the timeline is long enough.

## Why the early years look boring and the later years look dramatic

In the example above, Investor A's account is worth about $34,617 the day they stop contributing at 35 — after ten years of putting in $200 a month, the growth on top of their contributions is real but not yet dramatic. The next thirty years, where they add *nothing*, is where the account goes from $34,617 to $280,968. Compounding is heavily backloaded: the first years mostly reflect what you put in, and the later years mostly reflect what the earlier money has been quietly doing on its own. This is also exactly why cashing out early — even a "small" early withdrawal — costs more than the number on the statement suggests: you're not just removing that dollar amount, you're removing every year of growth it would have gone on to earn.

## The other side: compounding works against you too

The mechanism doesn't care whether it's working for you or against you. An [expense ratio]({{ '/what-is-an-expense-ratio/' | relative_url }}) is compounding running in reverse — a fee taken as a percentage of a growing balance, every year, compounding into a much larger loss than the percentage alone suggests. Credit card debt at 20%+ APR is the same mechanism at a punishing rate, working against a balance instead of for one, which is most of why carrying a balance is so much more expensive than the sticker interest rate makes it feel.

## What actually drives the outcome

Three things determine how much compounding does for you, and they're not equally controllable:

- **Time** — the single biggest lever, and the one the example above is built around. You can't get back a decade you didn't invest in, but you also can't run out of it until it's over, which is the case for starting today over waiting for a "better" moment.
- **Rate of return** — matters, but it's the least controllable of the three. Chasing a higher return usually means taking on more risk or more cost, and the [expense ratio]({{ '/what-is-an-expense-ratio/' | relative_url }}) post covers how a fee eats into this from the other direction.
- **Contribution amount** — matters, but as Investor A and B show, it doesn't dominate the other two the way people assume. More money in later can't fully substitute for time already spent compounding.

## The takeaway

Compound interest is interest on interest, and it's backloaded by nature — unremarkable for years, then suddenly not. The Investor A vs. Investor B numbers aren't a rounding trick: contributing a third as much money but starting a decade earlier produced a *larger* ending balance, because the extra decade of compounding was worth more than the extra two decades of contributions. Time is the input this blog can't hand you more of tomorrow than it can today, which is the actual, mathematical reason "start now" keeps showing up here in every form.
