"Roth IRA" gets thrown around like everyone's supposed to already know what it means, and then the explanations that follow are usually either too simplified to be useful ("it's for retirement!") or immediately fall into tax-code jargon about contribution limits and phase-outs. Here's the plain version.

## The one-sentence version

A Roth IRA is a special account for investing money where, if you follow the rules, you never pay tax on what it grows into — not now, not when you take it out in retirement, not ever on the gains.

That's the entire pitch. Everything else is detail.

<!--more-->

## How it's different from a regular brokerage account

A regular ("taxable") brokerage account and a Roth IRA can hold the exact same investments — same index funds, same stocks. The difference isn't what you can buy, it's the tax treatment:

- **Regular brokerage account:** you invest money you've already paid income tax on. Then, every year, you owe tax on dividends you receive, and tax on any gains when you sell (capital gains tax).
- **Roth IRA:** you also invest money you've already paid income tax on — that part's the same. But from there on, the account grows completely tax-free, and when you withdraw it in retirement (after age 59½, assuming the account's been open 5+ years), you owe **nothing** — not on the original contributions, not on decades of growth on top of them.

That second part is the whole point. A stock that grows from $1,000 into $10,000 inside a regular brokerage account owes capital gains tax on that $9,000 gain when you sell. The identical stock, growing the identical amount inside a Roth IRA, owes nothing.

## Why age matters here more than almost anywhere else

Because the benefit is "tax-free growth," and growth compounds over time, the value of a Roth IRA opened at 22 versus one opened at 35 isn't just "13 more years of contributions" — it's 13 more years of a much bigger balance growing completely untaxed. The earlier you open one, the more total growth ends up permanently shielded from tax, which is a bigger deal than most first-time explanations make it sound.

This is also why a Roth IRA is arguably a better first account for someone young and early-career than it looks on paper: you're very likely in a lower tax bracket right now than you will be later, which means paying tax on the money going in *today*, while your bracket is low, in exchange for zero tax on the (much larger) amount coming out later, is a genuinely good trade — one that gets worse the longer you wait to make it, since it only applies to contributions you make while you're still in a lower bracket.

## What you actually need to know to open one

- **You need earned income** (a job, including part-time or freelance work) to contribute — you can't fund a Roth IRA purely from, say, gift money with no income of your own.
- **There's an annual contribution limit** set by the IRS, adjusted periodically — it's a per-year cap on new contributions, not a cap on the account's total size (growth inside the account doesn't count against it).
- **There's an income limit at the higher end** — above a certain income, you can't contribute directly to a Roth IRA. This mostly doesn't affect someone early in their career, but it's worth knowing it exists before you assume it's available at every income level.
- **You can withdraw your contributions (not the growth) at any time, penalty-free** — this surprises people. The *growth* is what's locked in until retirement age to get the tax-free treatment; the money you personally put in stays accessible. That doesn't make it a good idea to treat it as a savings account, but it's not the irreversible lockbox people sometimes assume.
- **It's an account type, not an investment** — opening a Roth IRA and leaving the cash sitting in it uninvested does almost nothing. The account is the tax wrapper; you still have to choose what to invest in inside it (commonly a broad index fund, for the same reasons discussed in [why college students should start investing]({{ '/college-students-should-invest/' | relative_url }})).

## The honest caveats

A Roth IRA isn't universally the "correct" choice over its main alternative, a **Traditional IRA** (where contributions reduce your taxable income now, but withdrawals are taxed in retirement) — the right one depends on whether you expect to be in a higher or lower tax bracket in retirement than you are today, which nobody can know for certain in advance. For most people early in their career, still climbing toward their peak earning years, the Roth's bet (low bracket now, presumably higher later) tends to make sense — but "tends to" isn't "always," and this isn't tax advice for your specific situation.

Most major brokerages offer Roth IRAs with no account minimum and no added fees beyond what you'd pay in a regular account, so the barrier to opening one is closer to "filling out a form" than "having enough money to bother." That's the part worth not letting intimidate you out of it.
