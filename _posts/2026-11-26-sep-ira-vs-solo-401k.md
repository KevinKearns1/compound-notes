I wrote about [W-2 vs. 1099 income]({{ '/w2-vs-1099/' | relative_url }}) and, at the end, waved at "investing in a brokerage account or IRA" as if that covered it for self-employed earners. It doesn't. A regular IRA caps out at $7,500 for 2026 — fine for a W-2 employee stacking it on top of a 401(k), but a rounding error for someone whose 1099 income *is* the whole retirement plan. There are two accounts built specifically for self-employment income that go far beyond that, and the gap between them is bigger than it looks.

## The one-sentence version

A SEP-IRA and a solo 401(k) both let self-employed earners shelter far more than a regular IRA, but a solo 401(k) adds a second, separate contribution bucket that a SEP-IRA doesn't have — and for most freelancers, that second bucket is worth tens of thousands of dollars a year.

<!--more-->

## What both accounts have in common

Both are built for someone with self-employment income and no non-spouse employees — a freelancer, a consultant, a single-person LLC, a side business run alongside a W-2 job. Both let you contribute well beyond a regular IRA's limit. Both reduce your taxable income (in the traditional version) the same way a traditional 401(k) does. Neither one requires an employer in the traditional sense — you're both the business and the saver.

## Where they actually diverge

A **SEP-IRA** has one contribution bucket: an "employer" contribution of up to 20% of your net self-employment earnings (after the deduction for half your self-employment tax), capped at $72,000 for 2026. There's no separate employee-deferral piece — just the one number.

A **solo 401(k)** has two buckets stacked together: an **employee deferral** of up to $24,500 for 2026 (the same number a W-2 employee's 401(k) uses), *plus* an **employer contribution** of up to 20% of net self-employment earnings — the same formula a SEP-IRA uses for its one bucket. The combined total is capped at $72,000.

That second bucket is the entire story. Here's $80,000 in net self-employment profit, run through both:

| | SEP-IRA | Solo 401(k) |
|---|---|---|
| Self-employment tax | $11,303.64 (half-deduction: $5,651.82) | same |
| Net earnings for the formula | $74,348.18 | same |
| Employer-style contribution (20%) | **$14,869.64** | $14,869.64 |
| Employee deferral | — | **$24,500.00** |
| **Total max contribution** | **$14,869.64** | **$39,369.64** |

Same income, same business, same tax situation — **the solo 401(k) shelters $24,500 more**, because it has a bucket the SEP-IRA simply doesn't offer. That $24,500 gap isn't specific to $80,000 of profit, either: it holds at $150,000, $200,000, pretty much anywhere in between, because the employee deferral is a flat dollar cap, not a percentage. It only starts shrinking once the *combined* $72,000 ceiling binds — which for a sole proprietor happens right around $360,000 of net profit, where both accounts converge toward the same number because there's no more room left for either bucket to grow.

## Why the SEP-IRA still exists

Given that gap, it's a fair question why anyone opens a SEP-IRA at all. Two real reasons:

**It's simpler.** A SEP-IRA is close to as easy to open as a regular IRA — one form at most brokerages, no separate plan document, no annual filing obligations even as it grows. A solo 401(k) is still simple compared to a company plan, but it's a real plan: you adopt plan documents, and once its assets cross $250,000 you owe the IRS a short annual Form 5500-EZ.

**It fits irregular, low-conviction income.** If the self-employment income is a small, unpredictable side stream on top of a W-2 job — a few thousand dollars from freelance work some years and none in others — the simplicity of a SEP-IRA can be worth more than the extra shelter a solo 401(k) would add on income that small anyway.

For anyone with a meaningful, reasonably stable stream of self-employment income, though, the solo 401(k)'s extra bucket is hard to justify giving up.

## The one thing a solo 401(k) requires that a SEP doesn't

A solo 401(k) is only available if the business has **no employees other than you and a spouse** — the instant there's a non-spouse employee working enough hours to be plan-eligible, the solo 401(k) structure stops working and the business needs a different plan entirely. A SEP-IRA has more flexibility here (it can cover employees, with rules about contributing proportionally for them too), which matters the moment hiring is on the table, even informally.

## Catch-up contributions, if you're 50+

Both accounts track the same catch-up add-ons available to any 401(k) or IRA-style plan: workers 50 and older can add an extra $8,000 for 2026, and those aged 60-63 get a larger $11,250 catch-up instead. A SEP-IRA has no catch-up provision at all — another point in the solo 401(k)'s favor for an older freelancer trying to catch up on retirement savings in the years right before drawing it down.

## The deadline nuance worth knowing

A SEP-IRA has always been easy to set up and fund as late as your tax filing deadline (including extensions) for the *previous* year — useful if you're deciding in March whether last year's income justified opening one at all. Solo 401(k)s used to require being opened by December 31 of the tax year itself, which ruled them out for a late decision. That changed with SECURE 2.0: a sole proprietor can now generally establish a solo 401(k) retroactively, by their own tax filing deadline, and still count both the employee and employer pieces for the prior year. The exact deadline treatment for the employer-contribution piece varies by provider interpretation, so confirm the specifics with whoever holds the plan before assuming an extension applies — but the old "you had to decide by New Year's Eve" rule is no longer the dividing line it used to be.

## The takeaway

A SEP-IRA and a solo 401(k) aren't two flavors of the same thing — a solo 401(k) has a whole second contribution bucket that a SEP-IRA doesn't, worth roughly $24,500 a year at most realistic income levels, only converging once combined contributions approach $72,000 around $360,000 of net profit. For a meaningful, ongoing stream of 1099 income, the solo 401(k)'s extra complexity is a small price for the extra shelter. "A brokerage account or an IRA" was never really the ceiling for what self-employment income can do — it was just the version of this blog that hadn't gotten to this post yet.
