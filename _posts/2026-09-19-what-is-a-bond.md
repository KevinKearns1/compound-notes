I explained [what a stock actually is]({{ '/what-is-a-stock/' | relative_url }}) recently, and bonds are the other term that shows up constantly in investing advice ("a mix of stocks and bonds," "shift toward bonds as you get older") without much explanation of what a bond actually is. It's a much simpler concept than a stock, which is part of why it gets skipped over.

## The one-sentence version

A bond is a loan — you lend money to a government or a company, and in exchange they promise to pay you back the full amount on a specific date, plus regular interest payments along the way.

<!--more-->

## The mechanics, plainly

Say you buy a $1,000 bond with a 5% annual interest rate ("coupon rate") and a 10-year term. Here's what actually happens:

- You hand over $1,000 to the bond issuer (a company or government) today.
- Every year for 10 years, they pay you $50 (5% of $1,000) in interest.
- At the end of the 10 years, they pay you back the original $1,000.

That's the whole transaction. Unlike a stock, there's no ownership, no voting rights, no share of profits beyond what was promised upfront — a bond is a fixed, contractual promise to pay a specific amount on a specific schedule, not a claim on how well the company or government actually performs.

## Why bonds are considered "safer" than stocks

**The payment is contractual, not dependent on performance.** A company having a great year doesn't pay bondholders more, and a company having a bad year still owes bondholders the same fixed amount — right up until the company can't pay at all (default), which is the actual risk with a bond, not day-to-day price swings.

**Bondholders get paid before stockholders if a company fails.** I mentioned this in the [stock post]({{ '/what-is-a-stock/' | relative_url }}) — if a company goes bankrupt and its assets get liquidated, bondholders are in line ahead of shareholders. That doesn't make a bond risk-free, but it's a real structural advantage over stock in the same company.

**Prices move less dramatically.** A bond's value can still fluctuate before it matures (more on that below), but generally in a narrower range than a stock's price, which is why increasing a portfolio's bond allocation is the standard way to reduce overall volatility.

None of this makes bonds risk-free. A bond is only as safe as whoever issued it — a bond from the U.S. Treasury is about as safe as an investment gets; a bond from a financially shaky company carries real default risk, sometimes priced with a much higher interest rate specifically because of that risk ("junk bonds" or "high-yield bonds" are exactly this — higher promised return because the risk of not getting paid back is real).

## The part that confuses people: bond prices move too

Bonds aren't "buy and the value never changes until maturity" — if you sell a bond before its maturity date, you sell it at whatever price the market currently thinks it's worth, which moves opposite to interest rates in a specific, learnable way:

**When interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall.** If you're holding a bond paying 3% and new bonds start being issued at 5%, your 3% bond is less attractive by comparison — nobody will pay full price for it when they could buy a new bond paying more, so its market price drops to compensate. The reverse is also true: when rates fall, existing higher-rate bonds become more valuable.

This is why "bonds are safe" needs a footnote: they're safe in the sense of a predictable schedule of payments if held to maturity, but their market value can genuinely drop in the meantime, particularly when interest rates rise — 2022 was a real, recent example of bond prices falling meaningfully as rates rose quickly.

## Why a portfolio might include both

Stocks and bonds don't typically move in lockstep, and bonds' fixed, contractual payments provide a cushion stocks don't have. The classic reasoning for shifting toward more bonds as you approach a goal (like retirement) is about reducing how much a bad year could hurt you right when you need the money — you have less time to wait out a downturn, so trading some of stocks' higher expected return for bonds' relative stability becomes a more reasonable trade the closer you get.

## The takeaway

A bond is a loan with a name and a fancier wrapper — you lend money, you get paid interest on a schedule, you get your principal back at the end, assuming the borrower doesn't default. It's simpler than a stock in structure, safer in a specific, limited sense, and it's the thing "diversify with bonds" has actually been asking you to buy all along.
