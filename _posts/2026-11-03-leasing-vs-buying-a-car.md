I ran the actual numbers on [renting vs. buying a home]({{ '/renting-vs-buying-a-home/' | relative_url }}) and found the slogan version oversold how obvious the answer is. Leasing vs. buying a car gets the same treatment — "leasing is throwing money away" — and it has the same problem: the real math depends on one specific variable, and it flips the answer depending on which side of it you're on.

## The one-sentence version

Buying a car is cheaper than leasing if you keep it well past the loan payoff point and drive it for years essentially free; leasing can be cheaper, or close to it, if you trade in every few years anyway — because selling a financed car early means paying off a loan that's barely ahead of the car's own depreciation.

<!--more-->

## The short-horizon case: trading in every 3 years

Here's a $35,000 car, $2,000 down, compared two ways over exactly 3 years: a 36-month lease, versus financing the purchase (5-year loan at 6.5% APR) and selling it at the 3-year mark.

| | 3-year lease | Buy, financed, sold at 3 years |
|---|---|---|
| Total cash paid in | $18,101 | $25,245 |
| Value recovered at the end | $0 (hand back the keys) | $4,755 (resale value minus remaining loan) |
| **Net cost** | **$18,101** | **$20,489** |

**Leasing wins by about $2,388** in this scenario, and the reason is specific: after 3 years on a 5-year loan, you've only paid off a little over half the loan, while the car has already lost roughly 45% of its value. The resale value and the remaining loan balance are close together, so there's very little equity to recover — you're not "getting money back" by selling, you're mostly just closing out a loan that hasn't caught up to the car's depreciation yet.

## The long-horizon case: keeping it past the loan

Same car, same financing, but now compared over 6 years — two consecutive 3-year lease cycles versus buying once and keeping the car the full 6 years (paid off after year 5, driven free for the 6th).

| | Two 3-year leases | Buy once, keep 6 years |
|---|---|---|
| Total cash paid in | $36,203 | $40,741 |
| Value recovered at the end | $0 | $15,750 (resale value) |
| **Net cost** | **$36,203** | **$24,991** |

**Buying wins by about $11,212** here — the exact opposite result from the 3-year case, using the same car, the same loan terms, and the same depreciation assumptions. The only thing that changed is how long the car gets kept. Once the loan is paid off, every additional year of driving essentially costs only fuel, insurance, and maintenance — there's no more financing cost and no more trade-in math working against you.

## Why this isn't a contradiction

Both numbers are correct at the same time, because they're measuring different decisions. A lease is really a recurring short-term arrangement — you're paying for the *depreciation that happens during the years you have the car*, plus a finance charge, and nothing more. Buying is a long-term bet whose payoff only arrives once the loan is behind you — the first few years of ownership look expensive precisely because you're paying off the loan *and* absorbing the car's steepest depreciation at the same time, before any of the "drive it free" years show up to balance the ledger.

## What this means for the actual decision

**If you know you'll want a new car every 2-4 years regardless** — trading in, upgrading, or just preferring to always drive something under warranty — leasing is competitive on pure cost and comes with real side benefits the comparison above doesn't even count: no resale hassle, predictable fixed costs, and (usually) warranty coverage for the entire time you have the car, so unexpected repair costs are someone else's problem.

**If you plan to keep a car for 7-10+ years, well past when it's paid off** — buying wins decisively, and the gap widens every additional year you keep driving a fully-owned car. This is the scenario where "leasing is throwing money away" is actually true, just not for the reason the slogan usually gives (it's not that leasing "wastes" money each month — it's that it forfeits the free years a paid-off car eventually provides).

## The cost the math above doesn't include

**Mileage limits and wear-and-tear fees.** Most leases cap annual mileage (commonly 10,000-12,000 miles/year) and charge a per-mile fee for going over — often $0.15-$0.30 per mile, which adds up fast for anyone with a longer commute. Excess wear (beyond normal) at lease-end can also trigger separate charges. Someone who drives significantly more than the cap should run their own numbers with a realistic mileage estimate before assuming the lease math above applies to them — a high-mileage driver can turn a competitive lease into a clearly worse deal.

## The takeaway

Neither "always lease" nor "always buy" is honest advice — the entire comparison hinges on one knowable fact about yourself: roughly how long you actually plan to keep the car. Someone who trades in every few years isn't obviously losing money by leasing, and someone who drives a car for a decade isn't obviously being smart by financing it if they're going to trade it in after 3 years anyway. Match the ownership structure to your actual timeline, not to which option "sounds" more financially responsible.
