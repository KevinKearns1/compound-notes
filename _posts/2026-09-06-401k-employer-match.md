Somewhere between a fifth and a third of workers with access to a 401(k) match don't contribute enough to get the full amount, depending on which survey you look at. That's not a rounding error — that's a very large number of people turning down money their employer is actively trying to give them, usually without realizing that's what's happening.

## What a match actually is

A 401(k) is a retirement account offered through your employer. Many employers **match** a portion of what you contribute — a common structure is "we'll match 100% of the first 3% of your salary you contribute, then 50% of the next 2%." The exact formula varies by company, but the mechanism is always the same shape: **you put money in, your employer adds more on top of it, for free, as long as you contribute enough to trigger the match.**

<!--more-->

If you make $50,000 a year and your employer matches 100% of the first 3%, contributing that 3% ($1,500) gets you an *additional* $1,500 from your employer — deposited into your account, vested on whatever schedule your plan uses, for a decision that cost you nothing beyond redirecting money you were already earning. Skip that contribution, and that $1,500 doesn't come to you in any other form. It isn't rolled into your paycheck instead. It just doesn't happen.

## Why "I can't afford to contribute" often has it backwards

The instinct to skip 401(k) contributions when money is tight is understandable — it looks like giving up cash you need now. But it's worth running the actual math before deciding that, for two reasons:

1. **Contributions typically come out pre-tax**, which means contributing $100 doesn't reduce your take-home pay by the full $100 — it reduces it by $100 minus whatever income tax you would have paid on that $100. The real cost to your paycheck is smaller than the number on the contribution.
2. **The match is additional money you don't otherwise have access to.** Even setting aside the tax point, turning down a 50%-or-100%-instant-return on part of your paycheck is a worse deal than almost any other financial decision available to you — there's no investment, savings account, or debt payoff (short of very high-interest debt) that reliably beats an immediate 50-100% return.

None of this means max out your 401(k) before paying rent. It means **the amount required to get the full match** (often 3-6% of salary) deserves to be the very last thing cut from a budget, not the first — because unlike most budget line items, this one has a matching employer contribution attached to it that disappears the moment you contribute less.

## The one number to actually find

Every 401(k) plan has a specific match formula, and it's usually sitting in your employer's benefits portal or offer paperwork, described in a sentence like "100% match on the first 3%, 50% match on the next 2%." That sentence tells you exactly what percentage of your salary you need to contribute to get every dollar of matching money available to you — anything less than that number leaves part of the match unclaimed; anything at that number or above gets you all of it (contributing more doesn't get you *more* match, just more of your own money invested).

If you don't know your plan's match formula, that's the actual homework here — not "start saving more" in the abstract, but finding that one specific percentage and checking your current contribution rate against it.

## Vesting is the asterisk worth knowing about

Some employers use a **vesting schedule** — you may need to stay at the company for a certain number of years before the *employer's* contributions (not yours) fully belong to you. Leave early, and you can forfeit some or all of the unvested match. This doesn't mean the match isn't worth pursuing; it means it's worth knowing your plan's vesting schedule, especially if you're weighing a near-term job change, so you're not blindsided by which part of the balance actually leaves with you.

## The takeaway

A 401(k) match is one of the few places in personal finance where the "right" move is genuinely simple and doesn't depend on predicting the market, picking good investments, or having any particular risk tolerance — it just depends on knowing one percentage and contributing at least that much. Find the number. Meet it if you can. Everything else about investing is harder than this one is.
