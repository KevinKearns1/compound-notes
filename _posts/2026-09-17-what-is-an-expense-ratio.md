I've told you to buy [broad index funds]({{ '/index-funds-vs-individual-stocks/' | relative_url }}) a few times on here without explaining the one number that actually determines which specific fund you should pick once you're choosing between them. That number is the expense ratio, and the difference between a good one and a bad one is a lot bigger than the tiny percentage suggests.

## What it actually is

An expense ratio is the annual fee a fund charges, expressed as a percentage of your investment, taken automatically out of the fund's returns — you never see a separate bill, it's just quietly subtracted before you see your balance. A 0.50% expense ratio means the fund keeps 0.50% of your invested balance every year, regardless of whether the fund goes up or down that year.

<!--more-->

## Why a fraction of a percent isn't a rounding error

Expense ratios today range from around **0.02-0.05%** for the cheapest broad index funds to **1% or more** for some actively managed funds. That sounds like a trivial difference — we're talking about less than one percentage point either way. But it compounds against you every single year, on your whole balance, for as long as you hold the fund.

Here's $10,000 invested for 30 years at a 7% average annual return, before fees, compared at two expense ratios:

| Expense ratio | Value after 30 years | Amount lost to fees |
|---|---|---|
| 0.03% | ~$75,485 | ~$640 |
| 1.00% | ~$57,435 | ~$18,690 |

Same starting amount, same market performance, same 30 years. The only difference is the fee — and it's the difference between losing about $640 to fees and losing nearly $18,700. That's not a rounding error, that's close to a third of the entire ending balance in the higher-fee case, silently taken because nobody sent a bill.

## Why the gap is so much bigger than the percentage looks

The fee isn't just taken once — it's taken every year, on a balance that's supposed to be growing. A 1% fee doesn't cost you 1% of your original investment; it costs you 1% of your balance *every single year*, including all the growth that balance has accumulated. Money that gets skimmed off early doesn't just disappear — it disappears along with everything it would have compounded into over the following decades. That's the mechanism that turns "less than 1 percentage point" into tens of thousands of dollars over a long enough timeline.

## Why actively managed funds tend to cost more

Index funds are cheap because they're mechanical — the fund just holds whatever's in the index it tracks, no human deciding what to buy or sell. Actively managed funds pay a manager (and often a research team) to try to pick investments that beat the market, and that cost shows up as a higher expense ratio. The problem, which I touched on in the [index funds post]({{ '/index-funds-vs-individual-stocks/' | relative_url }}), is that actively managed funds frequently fail to beat the market even before their fees — and after their (much higher) fees, beating a comparable low-cost index fund over the long run is the exception, not the rule.

## Where to actually find this number

Every fund has to disclose its expense ratio, usually listed right on the fund's summary page wherever you're buying it (in Robinhood, most brokerages, or the fund provider's own site) — look for "expense ratio" or "ER" near the fund's basic stats. For a broad U.S. or total-market index fund, anything above roughly 0.10% is worth double-checking why — there are widely available options under 0.05% doing the same basic job.

## The takeaway

When two funds hold basically the same thing, the expense ratio is often the single biggest controllable factor in which one leaves you with more money decades from now — more than which specific fund "performed better" last year, which is mostly noise anyway. Check this number before buying any fund. It's small enough to ignore by accident and large enough to matter enormously if you do.
