I mentioned [borrowing against home equity]({{ '/extra-mortgage-payments-or-invest/' | relative_url }}) as one way to get cash back out of a paid-down mortgage, without explaining that there are two genuinely different products that do this — and one of them has a payment change built in that catches people off guard years after they signed up for it.

## The one-sentence version

A HELOC (home equity line of credit) is a revolving, usually variable-rate credit line you draw from as needed, typically interest-only for about the first 10 years before shifting into a roughly 20-year period of full principal-and-interest payments; a home equity loan is a fixed-rate, fixed-payment lump sum from day one, structured like a second mortgage — and mixing up which one you have changes what the monthly bill actually looks like and when it changes.

<!--more-->

## The two products, side by side

| | HELOC | Home equity loan |
|---|---|---|
| How you get the money | Draw as needed, up to a limit | One lump sum, upfront |
| Rate | Usually variable | Usually fixed |
| Early payments | Often interest-only during the draw period | Principal + interest from day one |
| Structure | Revolving credit, like a credit card | Installment loan, like a second mortgage |

Both borrow against the same thing — the equity built up in a home — but the shape of the obligation is completely different, and so is how the payment behaves over time.

## The payment shock, worked out

A HELOC's draw period (commonly around 10 years) is often interest-only — you're only required to pay the interest on whatever balance you've drawn, not reduce the principal at all. When the draw period ends, the loan shifts into a repayment period (commonly around 20 years) where the remaining balance starts amortizing like a normal loan — principal and interest both, on whatever balance exists at that point.

Here's a $50,000 balance at a representative 8% variable rate, comparing the last interest-only payment during the draw period against the first payment once repayment begins:

| | Monthly payment |
|---|---|
| Draw period (interest-only) | **$333** |
| Repayment period (20-year amortizing) | **$418** |

**That's a 25% jump — on the identical balance, at the identical rate** — just from the structure of the loan changing at a fixed point in time, often a decade after the HELOC was opened and easy to lose track of. And because HELOC rates are usually variable, a borrower who also sees their rate rise over that same decade faces both increases landing at once: a larger required payment *and* a higher rate, right as the loan shifts from interest-only to fully amortizing. A home equity loan has none of this — the payment calculated on day one is the payment for the life of the loan, full stop.

## How much either one actually offers

Lenders typically cap the **combined loan-to-value ratio (CLTV)** — the existing mortgage balance plus the new HELOC or home equity loan, compared to the home's value — at roughly **80-85%**. On a home worth $400,000 with $250,000 remaining on the mortgage, an 80% CLTV cap allows combined borrowing up to $320,000 — meaning roughly **$70,000** of new HELOC or home equity loan is potentially available, before accounting for credit, income, and the specific lender's own standards. This directly connects to [the affordability math]({{ '/how-much-house-can-you-afford/' | relative_url }}) covered earlier: how much equity actually exists to borrow against depends on the same loan balance and home value already part of that calculation.

## When each one actually fits

**A HELOC fits an uncertain or ongoing need** — a renovation with costs that aren't fully known upfront, or a flexible financial backstop drawn only if actually needed, paying interest only on what's actually used rather than a full lump sum sitting unused. It is **not** a substitute for an actual [emergency fund]({{ '/emergency-fund-how-much/' | relative_url }}) — approval and access can change or tighten exactly when a borrower's finances are under the most stress, which is the opposite of what an emergency fund needs to reliably do.

**A home equity loan fits a known, one-time expense** — debt consolidation, a single large known cost — where a fixed payment and a fixed rate from day one matter more than flexibility, and there's no ten-years-later structural change to plan around.

## The real risk both share

Both are secured against the home — a second lien, the same way the primary mortgage is secured. Falling behind on either one carries the same ultimate consequence as falling behind on the mortgage itself: the home is the collateral, and foreclosure is a real possibility, not a remote technicality. Borrowing against home equity for a discretionary expense deserves the same seriousness as the mortgage itself, not a lighter one just because the word "credit line" sounds more casual than "second mortgage."

## The takeaway

A HELOC and a home equity loan both unlock the same underlying value — equity built up in a home — through structurally different products with different payment behavior over time. The HELOC's built-in shift from interest-only to fully amortizing is the detail most likely to surprise a borrower years after closing, specifically because it's easy to forget a fixed draw period even exists when the payment feels stable for a decade. Knowing which product is actually in hand, and when the terms are scheduled to change, matters as much as the interest rate quoted on day one.
