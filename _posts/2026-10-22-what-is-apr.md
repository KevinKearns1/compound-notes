I've cited "20%+ APR credit card debt" twice on this blog — in the [budget rule post]({{ '/50-30-20-budget-rule/' | relative_url }}) and the [compound interest post]({{ '/what-is-compound-interest/' | relative_url }}) — without ever explaining what APR actually is, or showing what "just pay the minimum" actually does to a balance over time. The real math is worse than the sticker number suggests.

## The one-sentence version

APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is the yearly interest rate charged on a balance, applied in smaller pieces each billing cycle — and a credit card's minimum payment is deliberately small enough that making only the minimum can take years or decades to actually pay off a balance, most of it going to interest, not the debt itself.

<!--more-->

## What APR actually is, mechanically

A credit card's APR gets divided into a daily or monthly rate and applied to whatever balance you're carrying. A 22% APR translates to roughly a 1.83% monthly rate (22% ÷ 12) — charged on your balance every single month you carry one. Pay your statement balance in full every month, and APR is irrelevant to you entirely, since there's no balance left to charge it against. Carry a balance, and it compounds the exact way [any interest does]({{ '/what-is-compound-interest/' | relative_url }}), just working against you instead of for you.

## The minimum payment trap, with real numbers

Here's a $5,000 balance at 22% APR, with a minimum payment set at a common formula: **2% of the balance, or $25, whichever is greater**, recalculated each month as the balance changes:

| | Minimum payments only | Fixed $200/month |
|---|---|---|
| Time to pay off | **80.7 years** | 2.8 years |
| Total interest paid | **$43,419** | $1,750 |

That's not a typo. Because the minimum payment shrinks along with the balance, and most of each small payment goes toward interest rather than principal once the balance gets low, the payoff drags out for decades under this common minimum-payment structure — nearly nine times the original $5,000 balance, paid entirely in interest, for a debt that could have been cleared in under three years with a fixed, meaningfully larger payment.

The exact numbers vary by issuer's specific minimum-payment formula, but the shape is the same everywhere a minimum is calculated as a shrinking percentage of the balance: the payment gets smaller precisely as the balance does, which is the mechanical reason "just pay the minimum" can stretch a $5,000 debt out for most of a working lifetime.

## Why the minimum payment is deliberately small

A credit card issuer sets the minimum payment to keep an account in good standing while maximizing how long — and how much — interest it collects. There's no conspiracy required to explain this; it's simply the most profitable structure available to the issuer, and the structure happens to work against the cardholder's interest in paying off the balance quickly, which is exactly why "the minimum" shouldn't be read as "a reasonable amount to pay."

## Why this ties back to the 50/30/20 and compound interest posts

In the [budget rule post]({{ '/50-30-20-budget-rule/' | relative_url }}), I said paying off a 20% APR balance is "a guaranteed 20% return that no investment reliably matches" — the minimum-payment math above is the other half of that point: letting the balance sit at the minimum isn't a neutral choice, it's actively compounding against you at a rate most investments can't beat even on their best years. The [compound interest post]({{ '/what-is-compound-interest/' | relative_url }}) called this "compounding running in reverse" — this post is what that actually looks like in dollars and years, not just as a phrase.

## What actually fixes this

**Pay more than the minimum, as much as realistically possible**, every month — the fixed $200/month example above isn't a special trick, it's just a payment that doesn't shrink as the balance does, which is the entire mechanical reason it pays off in years instead of decades.

**Pay the highest-APR balance first** if carrying multiple cards (sometimes called the "avalanche" method) — mathematically, this minimizes total interest paid, since the highest rate is doing the most damage per dollar carried.

**Avoid adding new charges to a balance you're paying down** — new purchases typically start accruing interest immediately on a card already carrying a balance (the interest-free grace period on new purchases generally only applies when the previous statement was paid in full), so charging more while paying down an existing balance usually just grows the amount the high APR is working against.

## The takeaway

APR isn't an abstract number on a card's terms page — it's a real, compounding rate working against any balance you carry, and the minimum payment is structurally small enough that "paying the minimum" and "paying off the debt" are nearly opposite behaviors under a common minimum-payment formula. The fix isn't complicated: pay more than the minimum, as consistently and aggressively as the budget allows, because the gap between a shrinking minimum and a fixed real payment is the difference between decades and years.
