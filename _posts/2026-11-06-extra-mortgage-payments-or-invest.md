I worked through [student loans vs. investing]({{ '/pay-off-student-loans-or-invest/' | relative_url }}) with real numbers and specifically left mortgages out, promising they deserved their own answer. They do — a mortgage brings its own wrinkles a student loan doesn't have, and at a typical rate, the comparison is close enough that the actual math matters more than the instinct either way.

## The one-sentence version

Extra mortgage payments are a guaranteed, risk-free return equal to your mortgage rate; investing the same money instead has a higher *expected* return over the long run but isn't guaranteed — and at a typical mortgage rate, those two numbers are close enough that reasonable people doing the real math land on different sides.

<!--more-->

## The actual math

Here's a $300,000 mortgage balance at 6.5% (30-year), compared two ways over the same 17.5-year window: paying an extra $500/month toward principal, versus keeping the normal payment schedule and investing that same $500/month at a 7% average return instead.

| | Extra $500/mo to mortgage | Invest $500/mo instead |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome after 17.5 years | **$179,759 in interest saved** (guaranteed) | **$206,231 portfolio value** (not guaranteed) |

Investing comes out about **$26,500 ahead** in this comparison — but notice how close the two numbers are relative to the gap in the student loan comparison, where a 5% guaranteed payoff was being compared against the same ~7-8% expected return with a lot more daylight between them. A 6.5% mortgage rate against a 7% expected market return is a genuinely close call, and a mortgage even half a point higher or lower meaningfully shifts which side wins.

One more number worth sitting with: paying the extra $500/month also pays off the mortgage **12.5 years sooner** (17.5 years instead of 30) — a concrete, guaranteed timeline benefit that doesn't show up in the dollar comparison at all.

## Why "guaranteed" is doing a lot of work here

Paying down a mortgage early locks in a return exactly equal to your interest rate, with zero risk — every dollar of extra principal is a dollar of interest at that rate you will never pay. Investing instead is a bet on an *average* historical return that includes real years of losses along the way; the 7% figure is a long-run average, not a number any individual year reliably delivers. The comparison above assumes a smooth 7% every year, which never actually happens — a real investing path includes down years that a guaranteed mortgage payoff simply doesn't have to weather.

## The parts a student loan comparison doesn't have

**PMI removal, if it applies.** Anyone who put down less than 20% is likely paying private mortgage insurance (PMI) — typically 0.5-1% of the loan balance annually — until home equity crosses the 20% threshold. Extra payments that cross that line eliminate an ongoing cost on top of the interest savings, a guaranteed boost this comparison doesn't have an equivalent for in student debt.

**Liquidity, in the other direction.** Money paid into a mortgage is illiquid — getting it back out requires selling the home, refinancing, or borrowing against the equity (a home equity loan or line of credit), all of which take time and aren't guaranteed to be available on favorable terms when needed. Invested money (outside a retirement account) stays liquid and accessible, which matters for the same reason an [emergency fund]({{ '/emergency-fund-how-much/' | relative_url }}) needs to stay liquid — a dollar locked into home equity isn't a dollar you can reach quickly in a real emergency.

**The mortgage interest deduction, mostly doesn't apply anymore.** Mortgage interest is technically tax-deductible for people who itemize, which — since the standard deduction roughly doubled in 2018 — is now a minority of filers. Worth checking your own situation, but don't assume this tips the math; for most homeowners today, it doesn't meaningfully change the comparison.

## A reasonable way to actually split it

The same structure from the student loan post applies, in the same order:

1. Never skip the minimum mortgage payment to invest more — that's not optional.
2. Get the full [401(k) match]({{ '/401k-employer-match/' | relative_url }}) first, before extra mortgage payments — a 50-100% instant return beats any mortgage rate under nearly any analysis.
3. Beyond that, the split can reasonably scale with your specific rate: a mortgage under 4% (common for anyone who bought or refinanced in the early 2020s) leans hard toward investing; a rate at or above today's typical new-mortgage rates is a much closer call, often close to a coin flip.
4. If PMI removal is in reach with a modest extra payment, that's a clear, guaranteed near-term win worth prioritizing on its own, separate from the broader split.

## The takeaway

A mortgage isn't a student loan with a bigger balance — the typical rate sits close enough to long-run market returns that the "just invest, the math says so" answer that applies cleanly to a cheap loan doesn't apply as cleanly here, and the guaranteed, risk-free nature of paying down debt is worth something the dollar comparison alone doesn't fully capture. Get the match first, then decide based on your actual rate — not a general rule that was really written with a different kind of debt in mind.
