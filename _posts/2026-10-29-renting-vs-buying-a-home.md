"Renting is throwing money away" gets repeated so often it sounds like a settled financial law instead of an oversimplification. It isn't settled — the actual math depends heavily on numbers specific to your situation, and working through a real comparison usually lands closer than the slogan suggests.

## The one-sentence version

Renting isn't "wasted" money any more than buying is "free" money — buying trades a monthly rent payment for a mortgage payment plus taxes, insurance, maintenance, and a large upfront cost, in exchange for building home equity, while renting frees up that upfront cost and any monthly savings to be invested instead, and which option wins depends on specific numbers that vary by market and situation.

<!--more-->

## Why "throwing money away" misses half the picture

The slogan treats rent as pure loss and a mortgage payment as pure gain, but a mortgage payment isn't all building equity either — a meaningful share of it goes to interest, especially in the early years of a loan, the same way [carrying a credit card balance]({{ '/what-is-apr/' | relative_url }}) is mostly interest at first. And renting isn't pure loss: it frees up money — the down payment, closing costs, and often a lower monthly cost — that can go toward [investing]({{ '/what-is-a-brokerage-account/' | relative_url }}) instead of sitting in a house.

## A real, worked comparison

Here's a representative scenario, with every assumption stated: a $350,000 home, 20% down ($70,000) plus 3% closing costs ($10,500) — **$80,500 upfront**. A comparable rental costs $1,800/month; owning the same home (mortgage, property tax, insurance, maintenance) averages roughly $2,200/month — **$400/month more** than renting.

**The buy path:** after 10 years, assuming 3.5% annual home price appreciation and a 6.5% mortgage rate, the home is worth about **$493,710**, with roughly **$237,373** still owed — **about $256,336 in home equity.**

**The rent-and-invest path:** the renter invests the $80,500 they didn't spend upfront, plus the extra $400/month they didn't spend on ownership, at a 7% average return (the same [long-run benchmark]({{ '/what-is-compound-interest/' | relative_url }}) used elsewhere on this blog). After 10 years: **about $231,012.**

| | Buy | Rent + invest the difference |
|---|---|---|
| Net worth after 10 years | **~$256,336** | **~$231,012** |

Close — buying edges ahead by about $25,000 under these specific assumptions, which is a genuinely useful result: not a landslide for either side, and a clear demonstration that "renting wastes money" isn't the obvious truth it's presented as. Change the appreciation rate, the mortgage rate, the rent-vs-own monthly gap, or the time horizon, and this comparison can tip either direction.

## What actually swings this comparison

**How long you stay.** Buying has large upfront costs (closing costs, and selling costs later, commonly 5-6% of the sale price in agent commissions) that get spread over however long you own the home — staying 3 years makes those costs a much bigger drag per year than staying 15.

**The local rent-to-price ratio.** In some markets, rent is dramatically cheaper than owning the equivalent home; in others, it's close to the same or even more. This single number, specific to a specific city and property, probably matters more than any other variable in the comparison.

**Whether you'd actually invest the difference.** The rent-and-invest side of this math only works if the money that isn't going toward a mortgage actually gets invested — consistently, the way this blog has argued for [elsewhere]({{ '/dollar-cost-averaging/' | relative_url }}) — rather than just spent. A renter who doesn't invest the gap isn't comparing apples to apples; they're just not building equity in either direction.

**Maintenance and repair reality.** Owning comes with real, sometimes large, unpredictable costs (a roof, an HVAC system, a water heater) that a renter's landlord absorbs instead — the "average" maintenance estimate in any model is a smoothed number that can arrive as one large bill in a bad year.

## What buying genuinely offers beyond the math

Two real factors the spreadsheet doesn't fully capture: **payment stability** (a fixed-rate mortgage payment doesn't rise with inflation or a landlord's decision, while rent can) and **non-financial value** (the ability to renovate, the stability of not facing a lease non-renewal, the psychological weight of ownership) — these are legitimate parts of the decision, just not ones a dollar comparison captures.

## The takeaway

"Rent is throwing money away" is a slogan, not a calculation — the honest comparison, worked through with real numbers, often lands closer than either side expects, and genuinely depends on your specific local rent-to-price ratio, how long you'll stay, and whether the money freed up by renting would actually get invested rather than spent. Run your own numbers before accepting either side of this debate as settled — the math in this post reached a near-tie on purpose, because that's what an honest version of this comparison usually looks like.
