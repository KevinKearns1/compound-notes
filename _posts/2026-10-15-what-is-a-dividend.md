Dividends have come up in the [stock]({{ '/what-is-a-stock/' | relative_url }}), [capital gains]({{ '/capital-gains-tax-explained/' | relative_url }}), and [cost basis]({{ '/what-is-cost-basis/' | relative_url }}) posts without ever getting their own explanation. If "dividend yield" and "dividend investing" are terms you've nodded along to without fully pinning down, here's the real mechanism — and an honest read on whether chasing them is actually a strategy.

## The one-sentence version

A dividend is a portion of a company's profit paid out directly to shareholders, usually on a regular schedule, as a cash reward for owning the stock — separate from and in addition to whatever the stock's price does.

<!--more-->

## How it actually works

A company that's profitable can choose to pay some of that profit back to shareholders as a dividend, typically quarterly. Own 100 shares of a company paying $0.50 per share per quarter, and you receive $50 every quarter, deposited as cash into your [brokerage account]({{ '/what-is-a-brokerage-account/' | relative_url }}) — no action required, and it happens whether the stock's price went up, down, or sideways that quarter.

Not every company pays one. Many, especially younger or fast-growing companies, reinvest all their profit back into the business instead — growth over payout. Dividends tend to show up more with larger, more established, slower-growing companies that generate more cash than they currently need to reinvest.

## What "dividend yield" actually means

**Dividend yield** is the annual dividend expressed as a percentage of the current share price — it's the number that lets you compare dividend-paying stocks of wildly different prices on equal footing. A stock paying $3/year in dividends at a $100 share price has a 3% yield; the same $3 annual dividend at a $50 share price would be a 6% yield. The yield moves with the stock's price even if the dividend itself never changes, which is worth remembering before reading too much into a yield number in isolation.

## The two ways to actually use a dividend

**Take it as cash** — it lands in your account and you can spend it or use it for anything else, same as any other cash.

**Reinvest it (DRIP — dividend reinvestment plan)** — instead of taking the cash, it automatically buys more shares of the same stock, which then earn their own dividends going forward. This compounds in a very literal, visible way.

Here's the difference over 20 years, starting with 100 shares at $50, a 3% yield, and 5% annual price growth — comparing reinvesting every dividend versus taking the dividends as cash and never adding them back:

| | Reinvested (DRIP) | Taken as cash |
|---|---|---|
| Shares after 20 years | 144.8 | 100 |
| Ending value | **$19,209** | $13,266 |

Same stock, same price growth, same dividend — **$5,943 more** purely from letting each dividend buy more shares instead of walking away with the cash each time. This is the same [compounding]({{ '/what-is-compound-interest/' | relative_url }}) mechanism from every other post on here, just visibly running through a dividend instead of price growth alone.

## Why dividends aren't "free money"

This is the part dividend-focused marketing tends to skip: **a dividend payment isn't new wealth appearing from nowhere.** When a company pays a dividend, its stock price typically drops by roughly the dividend amount on the payment date — the company is literally handing out cash it held, so the company (and therefore the stock) is worth that much less the moment after paying it. You're not getting something in addition to your investment's value; you're converting a slice of your investment's value from "unrealized stock price" into "cash in hand." Real, useful, and often tax-relevant — but not a bonus on top of what you already owned.

## Qualified vs. ordinary dividends, briefly

I touched on this in the [capital gains post]({{ '/capital-gains-tax-explained/' | relative_url }}): **qualified dividends** (most dividends from stock held long enough) get the lower long-term capital gains tax rates; **ordinary (non-qualified) dividends** are taxed at your regular income tax rate, the same as short-term gains. The distinction mostly comes down to how long you've held the stock paying it — a detail worth knowing, not something you typically need to calculate yourself, since brokerages report which category each dividend falls into.

## Is chasing high dividend yields actually a strategy?

Worth being honest here: a very high yield is often a warning sign, not a bargain. A stock's yield can spike because its price has dropped sharply (the same dividend divided by a much lower price), which sometimes means the market expects that dividend to get cut soon — a company in real trouble, not a generous one. Chasing the highest yield you can find, without looking at why it's that high, is a common beginner trap that selects for distressed companies rather than strong ones.

A more grounded approach: dividends are one real, legitimate return a stock can provide, worth factoring into the total picture (dividend plus price growth) rather than treating as the whole strategy on its own. A broad [index fund]({{ '/etf-vs-mutual-fund-vs-index-fund/' | relative_url }}) holding thousands of companies, some of which pay dividends and some of which don't, already captures this without requiring you to hand-pick high-yield stocks and second-guess why their yield is high.

## The takeaway

A dividend is real cash, paid on a schedule, funded by company profit — and genuinely powerful when reinvested over time, the same compounding story as everywhere else on this blog. It's not free money layered on top of your investment's value, and a suspiciously high yield is more often a red flag than a reward. Treat it as one real piece of a stock's total return, not a signal to chase on its own.
