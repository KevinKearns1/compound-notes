I've written about [high-yield savings accounts]({{ '/high-yield-savings-accounts/' | relative_url }}) and [CDs]({{ '/cd-vs-high-yield-savings/' | relative_url }}) as places for safe cash to sit, both offered by banks. There's a third option that doesn't come from a bank at all — issued directly by the U.S. Treasury, with a rate formula built around inflation itself, and a set of access rules that make it a genuinely different tool, not just a third flavor of the same thing.

## The one-sentence version

A Series I savings bond is a U.S. government-backed savings product whose interest rate is a composite of a fixed rate (locked in at purchase) and an inflation rate (reset every six months based on measured inflation), which means — unlike a bank-set HYSA or CD rate — it's formula-driven directly off inflation, in exchange for real restrictions on how soon and how much you can access.

<!--more-->

## How the rate actually works

The **composite rate** has two parts. The **fixed rate** is set when you buy the bond and stays the same for the bond's entire life (up to 30 years) — currently, and for much of recent history, this component has been small, sometimes close to zero. The **inflation rate** component resets every six months (each May and November) based on measured inflation over the preceding period, and it's this half that does most of the work. The combined composite rate is what your money actually earns.

The mechanical difference from a HYSA or CD matters: a bank sets its savings or CD rate based on its own cost structure and the broader interest-rate environment — related to inflation, but not a direct formula. An I bond's rate is explicitly built from the inflation number itself. Illustrating the mechanism (not a quoted current rate, since it resets twice a year and should be checked directly with the Treasury before buying): in a year with 2% measured inflation, $10,000 might grow to roughly $10,210; in a year with 7% measured inflation, the same $10,000 might grow to roughly $10,710 — the bond's return tracks whatever inflation actually does, rather than whatever a bank chooses to offer that quarter.

## The real restrictions — this isn't just "a better HYSA"

**A $10,000 per person, per calendar year purchase limit** (for the standard electronic bonds bought directly through Treasury). This is a real ceiling — unlike a HYSA, which has no deposit limit, an I bond can only ever hold a bounded amount of anyone's savings, which immediately makes it a complement to other safe-cash accounts, not a full replacement for them.

**A mandatory one-year minimum hold.** The money is genuinely inaccessible for the first 12 months — not a penalty-but-available situation like an early CD withdrawal, a hard lock. This makes I bonds a poor fit for money that might be needed on short notice, including most [emergency fund]({{ '/emergency-fund-how-much/' | relative_url }}) money.

**A 3-months'-interest penalty for cashing out between years 1 and 5.** Similar in spirit to [a CD's early withdrawal penalty]({{ '/cd-vs-high-yield-savings/' | relative_url }}), though smaller in most cases — redeem before the 5-year mark and the most recent three months of interest are forfeited. After 5 years, there's no penalty at all, and the bond keeps earning (and remaining redeemable penalty-free) for up to 30 years total.

## The three-way comparison

| | HYSA | CD | I bond |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rate set by | Bank, market-driven | Bank, locked at purchase | Treasury formula, tied to inflation |
| Access | Immediate | Locked for the term (penalty to break early) | Locked 1 year minimum; penalty through year 5 |
| Deposit limit | None | None | $10,000/person/year |
| Issuer | FDIC-insured bank | FDIC-insured bank | U.S. Treasury (direct government backing) |

None of the three is strictly better — they answer different questions. A HYSA is for money that might be needed any day. A CD is for a known, fixed timeline with a locked rate. An I bond is specifically for money that can sit for at least a year, ideally longer, where protection against inflation eroding its value matters more than maximum flexibility.

## How to actually buy one

Unlike everything else covered on this blog, I bonds aren't bought through a [brokerage account]({{ '/what-is-a-brokerage-account/' | relative_url }}) — they're purchased directly from the federal government through its own direct-purchase website, a platform separate from any bank or broker. That's a genuinely different purchase process than anything else in this blog's "where to put money" posts, and worth knowing going in so it doesn't feel like a dead end when a familiar brokerage search doesn't turn them up.

## Who this is actually for

Someone with safe cash they won't need for at least a year — a portion of a house down payment fund still a couple of years out, money set aside for a known future expense, or simply a slice of a conservative portfolio looking for explicit inflation protection without taking on stock market risk. It's a poor fit for an emergency fund specifically because of the one-year lockup, and the $10,000/year limit means it's never going to be the *only* place safe money lives — it's one more tool in the same category as a HYSA and a CD, not a universal replacement for either.

## The takeaway

Series I savings bonds solve a specific problem neither a HYSA nor a CD solves directly: a rate that's formula-tied to inflation itself, backed by the federal government, for money that can genuinely sit untouched for at least a year. The $10,000 annual limit and the one-year lockup mean this was never going to be where most of anyone's safe cash lives — but for the slice of savings that fits those constraints, it's worth knowing this option exists outside the usual bank-account comparison.
