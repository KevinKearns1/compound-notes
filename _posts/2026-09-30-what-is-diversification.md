I've said "diversify" and "don't put all your eggs in one basket" more than once on this blog without ever answering the actual practical question underneath it: how many stocks does it actually take before you're meaningfully diversified? The honest answer surprises most people in both directions.

## The one-sentence version

Diversification means spreading your money across enough different investments that no single one failing can seriously damage your total portfolio — and it works because different companies (and sectors, and countries) don't all move together, so losses in one place tend to get offset by stability or gains somewhere else.

<!--more-->

## What diversification is actually doing, mechanically

Every individual stock carries two kinds of risk. **Company-specific risk** is the risk that's unique to that one business — a bad product launch, a lawsuit, a scandal, a competitor eating its market share. **Market risk** is the risk that affects basically everything at once — a recession, rising interest rates, a broad market downturn.

Diversification can eliminate the first kind almost entirely and does nothing at all about the second. Owning 50 unrelated companies instead of 1 doesn't protect you from a recession that drags the whole market down — it protects you from any *one* of those 50 companies having its own bad year sinking your entire portfolio with it.

## So how many stocks does it actually take?

This is the part with a real, researched answer, and it's more than most people guess: studies going back decades have generally found that somewhere in the range of **20-30 individual stocks**, spread across different sectors, captures most of the diversification benefit available from picking individual stocks — beyond that point, adding more names keeps helping, but with rapidly diminishing returns.

The catch: this only works if the 20-30 stocks are actually spread across different, unrelated sectors. Twenty tech stocks isn't diversification in any meaningful sense — as I put it in an [earlier post]({{ '/investing-lessons-learned-late/' | relative_url }}), that's "a concentrated bet on one sector wearing a diversified costume." True diversification needs different industries, different company sizes, and ideally some exposure outside just one country's economy.

## Why "just buy an index fund" sidesteps the whole question

This is the actual reason [broad index funds]({{ '/index-funds-vs-individual-stocks/' | relative_url }}) get recommended so heavily for beginners: a total-market index fund doesn't hold 20-30 companies, it holds thousands, across every major sector, automatically — you get the diversification benefit maxed out in a single purchase, without having to research or track dozens of individual positions yourself. Picking 25 well-researched stocks across different sectors *can* get you most of the way to the same diversification benefit, but it takes real, ongoing effort to build and maintain correctly. An index fund gets you there by default.

## What under-diversified actually looks like in practice

It's not always "I only own one stock." The more common versions:

- **Owning several stocks that are all correlated** — several bank stocks, or several tech stocks, that all tend to rise and fall together because they're exposed to the same underlying risks.
- **A portfolio dominated by employer stock** — common when a large chunk of compensation comes as stock in the company you work for. Your paycheck and a big share of your investments are now both tied to the same employer's fortunes, which is a specific, well-known concentration risk worth actively managing.
- **A "diversified-looking" portfolio that's secretly concentrated** — five different funds that all happen to track the same or overlapping indexes isn't five times the diversification, it's mostly the same exposure five times over.

## What diversification doesn't do

It's worth being honest about the limits. Diversification doesn't protect against a broad market decline — a total-market index fund still drops when the whole market drops, just not as sharply as any single struggling company might. It doesn't guarantee a positive return. And over-diversifying can quietly work against you too: past a certain point, adding more overlapping funds mostly adds complexity and fees without adding real additional protection.

## The takeaway

"Diversify" isn't a vague virtue, it's a specific, researchable question with a real answer: roughly 20-30 well-spread individual stocks gets you most of the practical benefit, or a broad index fund gets you effectively all of it in one step. If you're holding fewer than a handful of positions, or several positions that all rise and fall together, that's the actual, checkable definition of "not diversified" — not a feeling, a number you can count.
