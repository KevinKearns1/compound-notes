I said a genuinely complex financial situation "usually still benefits from a human" advisor without ever explaining the detail that actually matters most once you're looking for one: how that advisor gets paid determines, legally, whose interest they're required to put first.

## The one-sentence version

A **fee-only** advisor is paid only by the client and must act as a fiduciary — legally required to put the client's interest first — at all times; a **fee-based** advisor (a confusingly similar name) can also earn commissions and can switch between that fiduciary standard and a looser one depending on the specific transaction; a **commission-based** advisor is paid by the financial products they sell and is held only to the lower "suitability" standard.

<!--more-->

## The real legal distinction: fiduciary vs. suitability

The **fiduciary standard** requires an advisor to recommend what's actually best for the client, full stop. The **suitability standard** only requires a recommendation to be *appropriate* — not necessarily the best available option, just one that reasonably fits the client's situation. The textbook example of the gap between the two: a high-commission annuity can be perfectly "suitable" for a client even when a low-cost index fund would clearly serve them better — suitability doesn't require comparing the two and picking the better one, only clearing a lower bar on its own.

## The three pay structures

**Fee-only** — paid exclusively by the client, through a flat fee, an hourly rate, or a percentage of assets under management (AUM). No commissions from any product. Fee-only advisors registered with the SEC are required to act as fiduciaries.

**Commission-based** — paid by the companies whose products they sell (insurance policies, certain mutual funds, annuities), not by the client directly. Held to the suitability standard, not the fiduciary standard, which means the built-in incentive is to recommend whatever pays the advisor more, as long as it's still "suitable."

**Fee-based** — the one that causes the most confusion, specifically because the name sounds like "fee-only." A fee-based advisor charges client fees *and* can also earn commissions on products sold — meaning the same advisor can be a fiduciary while giving general advice, then shift to the lower suitability standard the moment they're recommending a specific commission-paying product. This dual-standard structure is legal and common, and it's exactly why "fee-based" and "fee-only" deserve to be read as two completely different things, not minor variations on the same phrase.

## What an ongoing AUM fee actually costs

Even a fee-only advisor charging a percentage of assets under management is a real, compounding cost — not a one-time fee. Here's $100,000 invested for 30 years at a 7% gross return, the same framing [used for fund expense ratios]({{ '/what-is-an-expense-ratio/' | relative_url }}) elsewhere on this blog, compared with and without a representative 1% annual AUM fee:

| | Ending balance after 30 years |
|---|---|
| No ongoing advisory fee | **$761,226** |
| 1% AUM fee/year (net 6% return) | **$574,349** |

**That's a difference of about $186,900 — nearly a quarter of what the no-fee balance would have grown to** — from a fee that sounds small stated as "just 1% a year." This isn't an argument against ever using an advisor; it's the same point the expense-ratio post made about fund costs, applied to advisory fees instead: a small annual percentage compounds into a genuinely large number over decades, and it's worth knowing the exact figure before agreeing to it.

## When paying for a human advisor still makes sense

The honest case for this hasn't changed: a genuinely complex situation — business ownership, estate planning, multiple competing financial goals — can benefit from a person who asks questions a [robo-advisor's]({{ '/what-is-a-robo-advisor/' | relative_url }}) questionnaire can't. The pay-structure question isn't whether to use an advisor, it's which kind — and for most people in that situation, a **fee-only, fiduciary-at-all-times** advisor is the structure worth specifically seeking out, since it removes the built-in incentive problem the other two structures carry.

## The one question worth asking directly

**"Are you a fiduciary at all times, for every recommendation you make to me?"** A fee-only advisor should answer yes without hesitation. A fee-based advisor may genuinely answer "yes, except when..." — and that exception is the entire point worth understanding before agreeing to work with them. A commission-based advisor isn't bound by the fiduciary standard at all, which doesn't make them untrustworthy, but does mean their incentives and the client's interests aren't structurally aligned the way a fee-only relationship's are.

## The takeaway

"Fee-based" and "fee-only" sound like two shades of the same thing and aren't — one is a confusing middle category that can legally shift between standing up for the client and selling them something, while the other has one standard, all the time. For anyone deciding a complex enough situation genuinely needs a human advisor, how that advisor gets paid isn't a minor detail to check later — it's the single fact that determines whose interest they're actually required to prioritize.
