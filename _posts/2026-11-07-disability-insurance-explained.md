I [wrote about life insurance]({{ '/term-vs-whole-life-insurance/' | relative_url }}) without covering a related but very different kind of coverage: insurance that protects your income if you're still alive but unable to work. It gets skipped far more often than life insurance does, and the reasons people skip it are mostly based on assumptions that don't hold up.

## The one-sentence version

Disability insurance replaces a portion of your income if an illness or injury — not necessarily a workplace accident — leaves you unable to work, and it's commonly skipped because of two specific, incorrect assumptions: that disability mainly means a workplace injury, and that Social Security would cover it if it happened.

<!--more-->

## The actual risk, from the source most people underestimate

The Social Security Administration's own disability-facts materials put it plainly: more than one in four of today's 20-year-olds will become disabled before reaching retirement age. That's not a niche risk being oversold by an insurance product — it's a government agency's own estimate of how often this happens to the general working population, long before anyone is near retirement.

## Misconception 1: "disability" means a workplace accident

Workers' compensation covers injuries that happen on the job — it's narrow by design. Most long-term disabilities aren't work-related at all: back injuries, cancer, cardiovascular conditions, and other illnesses account for the large majority of long-term disability claims, not workplace accidents. Someone who assumes "I don't have a dangerous job, so I don't need this" is answering a question disability insurance was never actually asking.

## Misconception 2: "Social Security would cover me"

Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) exists, but it's a much higher bar than most people assume. To qualify, you generally have to be unable to perform **any** substantial gainful work — not just your specific job, but any reasonably comparable work in the economy — and the approval process is slow, with a large share of initial applications denied and appeals that can take months to years to resolve. SSDI is a real backstop for severe, long-term cases, but it was never designed to be a primary income-replacement plan, and treating it as one leaves a real gap for anything less than total, long-term incapacity.

## Short-term vs. long-term disability

**Short-term disability (STD)** covers a shorter gap — commonly weeks to a few months — often with little to no waiting period before benefits start. It's built for things like a surgery recovery or a temporary medical leave.

**Long-term disability (LTD)** covers an extended inability to work, typically kicking in after a waiting period (commonly around 90 days) once short-term coverage or paid leave runs out, and can continue for years or until retirement age depending on the policy. This is the coverage doing the heavy lifting for the scenario that actually threatens someone's finances — an extended period with no income and no clear end date.

## What employer-provided coverage usually gets you (and misses)

Many employers offer group disability coverage, which is a real benefit worth checking for — but it commonly has two gaps worth knowing about. First, the benefit typically replaces only **60% or so of base salary**, not the full amount, and is often capped at a maximum dollar benefit regardless of income. Second, the tax treatment depends on who paid the premium: if your employer pays the premium (the common default), the benefit is generally taxable income when you receive it — shrinking that 60% further after tax. If you pay the premium yourself with after-tax dollars (sometimes offered as an employee-paid option during open enrollment), the benefit is generally received tax-free. That's a real, checkable choice during open enrollment, not a minor detail — it changes what the coverage is actually worth if it's ever used.

## The actual cost, relative to what it protects

Representative numbers: on a $70,000 income, a typical long-term disability policy replacing 60% would pay around **$42,000/year** if a claim is needed — and individual supplemental coverage commonly runs in the range of **1-3% of income annually** (roughly $700-$2,100/year on that same income). Five years of that benefit, if actually needed, is **$210,000** — funded by a premium that's a small fraction of a single year of the income it protects. This is the same asymmetry that makes [term life insurance]({{ '/term-vs-whole-life-insurance/' | relative_url }}) worth having for most people with dependents: a modest, predictable cost against a risk that's financially catastrophic if it happens and isn't covered.

## Who this matters most for

**Anyone whose income would disappear or drop sharply without a paycheck** — which is most people without a large [emergency fund]({{ '/emergency-fund-how-much/' | relative_url }}) or other income sources. It matters especially for the self-employed and [1099 contractors]({{ '/w2-vs-1099/' | relative_url }}), who have no employer group policy to fall back on at all and need to arrange individual coverage directly if they want any disability protection beyond personal savings.

## The takeaway

Disability insurance protects against a risk that's more common than most people assume, misunderstood in two specific and correctable ways — it's not just about workplace accidents, and Social Security isn't a reliable substitute — and relatively inexpensive against the income it protects. Check what your employer actually offers, whether you have the option to pay the premium yourself for tax-free benefits, and whether the coverage amount is realistic against your actual expenses — not whether you have a job that feels "safe," which isn't really what this insurance is protecting against.
