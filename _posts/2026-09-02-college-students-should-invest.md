Nobody tells college students to invest, and it's easy to see why. You're broke, tuition is eating whatever you do have, and "invest early" sounds like advice for someone with a salary, not someone splitting a pizza four ways to save six dollars. But the advice is aimed at you specifically — more than it's aimed at almost anyone else who will ever hear it.

Here's the part that doesn't get said enough: **the amount you start with barely matters. The age you start at matters enormously.**

## The math nobody shows you

Investing returns compound — the money you make starts making its own money, which starts making its own money. That effect is small in year one and enormous by year thirty, which means the single biggest lever you have isn't how much you invest, it's how many years you give it to compound.

Here's what putting away $50 a month looks like depending on when you start, assuming a long-run average return of about 7% a year — a common historical benchmark for a diversified U.S. stock portfolio, not a promise about what will actually happen:

| Start age | Years invested | Total you put in | Value at 65 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 45 | $27,000 | ~$189,000 |
| 30 | 35 | $21,000 | ~$90,000 |
| 40 | 25 | $15,000 | ~$40,500 |

Same $50 a month. Same assumed return. The only variable is when you started. Starting at 20 instead of 30 doesn't just add a decade of contributions — it more than doubles the end result, because that first decade has thirty-five more years to compound on top of it than any single dollar put in at 30 will ever get.

This is also why "I'll start investing once I have real money" is the most expensive sentence in personal finance. The version of you with real money in ten years will never be able to buy back these ten years. Nobody can.

## You don't need a job or a windfall

A few things that trip people up before they even start:

- **You don't need a lump sum.** Most brokerages let you invest with no account minimum and buy fractional shares, meaning $20 can buy a slice of an index fund instead of sitting in a savings account waiting to become "enough."
- **You don't need to pick stocks.** A broad index fund (something tracking the overall U.S. or global stock market) is a completely reasonable place to start, and it's what most professional advice converges on for a first investment — not because it's exciting, but because it doesn't require you to guess which individual company wins.
- **You don't need to be debt-free first, necessarily.** The usual order of operations is: build a small emergency cushion, pay off high-interest debt (credit cards, not federal student loans), then invest. But if you have $20 spare after that, it's worth putting to work rather than letting it sit.
- **You do need to actually not touch it.** Money you're investing for retirement or "someday" is not the same bucket as money for rent next month. Investing money you'll need in six months turns a long-term growth tool into a short-term gamble.

## How to actually open an account

The mechanical part is the easy part — it's a form, an ID, and a bank transfer, same as opening any bank account. Most major brokerages (Fidelity, Schwab, Robinhood, and others) let you open an individual brokerage account online in about ten minutes with no minimum deposit required to get started.

Whichever brokerage you choose, the actual first move is the same: set up a small, automatic, recurring transfer — even $20 a month — into a broad index fund, and then leave it alone. The goal at 20 isn't to get rich by 25. It's to make investing a background habit, the same way you'd set up autopay on a phone bill, so that by the time you do have real income, you're not starting from zero and you're not starting from scratch on the habit either.

## The one thing to actually remember

You will not regret starting small. You will, statistically, regret waiting — not because $20 a month will make you rich, but because the version of this habit that compounds for 45 years looks completely different from the version that compounds for 25, and the only cost of getting the longer version is starting now instead of later.
