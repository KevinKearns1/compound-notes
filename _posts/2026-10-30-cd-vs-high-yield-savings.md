I've written about [high-yield savings accounts]({{ '/high-yield-savings-accounts/' | relative_url }}) as the fix for cash sitting in a low-interest account, without ever covering the other common option for the same money: a certificate of deposit. The two get compared constantly, and the real difference isn't really about which one pays more — it's about what you're trading away to get a locked-in rate.

## The one-sentence version

A certificate of deposit (CD) is a savings product where you lock up a set amount of money for a fixed term (3 months, 1 year, 5 years) at a fixed interest rate, in exchange for giving up the ability to withdraw it penalty-free before that term ends — unlike a [high-yield savings account]({{ '/high-yield-savings-accounts/' | relative_url }}), which pays a variable rate but stays fully liquid the whole time.

<!--more-->

## What's actually happening, mechanically

You deposit money and choose a term. The bank pays you a fixed [APY]({{ '/what-is-apr/' | relative_url }}) for that exact term, guaranteed not to change no matter what happens to interest rates in the broader economy. In return, that money is meant to stay put until the term ends (the "maturity date") — pull it out early and you pay an early withdrawal penalty, typically calculated as a number of months' or days' worth of interest, not a flat fee. Like a HYSA, a CD from a bank is FDIC-insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank — the same safety, just a different access tradeoff.

## The actual tradeoff, in real numbers

Here's $10,000 in each, for 12 months, assuming rates stay exactly where they started: a 12-month CD at 4.75% APY, and a HYSA at 4.50% APY.

| | CD (4.75%, locked) | HYSA (4.50%, variable) |
|---|---|---|
| After 12 months | **$10,475** | **$10,450** |

A $25 edge for the CD — real, but small, and it's not really what a CD is *for*. The actual case for a CD shows up when rates move. Say the HYSA's rate drifts down from 4.50% to 3.00% partway through the year (a realistic scenario — HYSA rates move with the broader rate environment, as covered in the [HYSA post]({{ '/high-yield-savings-accounts/' | relative_url }})), while the CD, locked in at account opening, is unaffected:

| | CD (4.75%, locked, unaffected) | HYSA (starts 4.50%, drifts to 3.00% at month 6) |
|---|---|---|
| After 12 months | **$10,475** | **$10,378** |

Now the gap is **$97** instead of $25. **A CD isn't a bet that it pays more — it's a bet that the rate you lock in today won't look worse than what's available later.** When rates are falling, that bet pays off. When rates are rising, a HYSA's rate rises right along with them and a CD opened too early misses out — the CD holder is stuck at the old, now-below-market rate for the rest of the term.

## The part that actually matters: breaking one early

This is where the real cost hides, and where "CD pays more" stops being the whole story. A typical 12-month CD's early withdrawal penalty is **90 days of interest** — not 90 days of your *actual* gain, 90 days of interest calculated on the full balance, deducted from whatever you've accrued so far. Here's withdrawing that same $10,000 CD (4.75% APY) early, at three different points, compared to what the same money would have earned sitting in the 4.50% HYSA for the identical period:

| Withdraw at | Interest accrued | 90-day penalty | **Net from the CD** | HYSA over same period |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Month 3 | $118.75 | $118.75 | **$0.00** | $112.50 |
| Month 6 | $237.50 | $118.75 | **$118.75** | $225.00 |
| Month 9 | $356.25 | $118.75 | **$237.50** | $337.50 |

Breaking the CD at month 3 nets **exactly $0** — every dollar of interest earned, erased by the penalty, with the hassle of an early withdrawal and none of the upside. At month 6 and month 9, the CD still comes out behind the HYSA for the exact same period, because the penalty eats a fixed chunk regardless of how long you actually held it. **A CD only wins its own bet if you make it to maturity.** Break it early, and the account that was fully liquid the entire time comes out ahead almost every time.

## The practical middle ground: CD ladders

If the appeal of a CD's locked rate is real but the all-or-nothing liquidity isn't, a **CD ladder** splits the difference: instead of one $10,000 CD maturing in 12 months, split it into several smaller CDs maturing at staggered intervals (say, $2,500 each maturing in 3, 6, 9, and 12 months). Something matures every few months, giving you periodic access without needing to break any single CD early, while most of the money still earns the locked-in rate. It's more setup than a single account, but it directly answers the "what if I need some of this before the term is up" problem instead of betting the entire balance on not needing it.

## When each one actually makes sense

**A HYSA makes sense** for money that needs to stay genuinely liquid — the [emergency fund]({{ '/emergency-fund-how-much/' | relative_url }}) money, or any cash goal where "I might need this on short notice" is a real possibility, not a hypothetical.

**A CD makes sense** for money with a known, fixed timeline — you know you won't need it until a specific date (a house down payment 18 months out, a tax bill due next spring) — and you want certainty about the rate rather than exposure to whatever the broader rate environment does between now and then. The defining question isn't "which pays more," it's "am I certain I won't need this before the term ends" — and if the honest answer is "probably, but not certain," the penalty math above says that uncertainty is expensive.

## The takeaway

A CD isn't a better savings account than a HYSA — it's a different trade: a locked-in rate in exchange for locked-up access, and that trade only pays off if you actually hold it to maturity. The penalty for guessing wrong isn't trivial; breaking a 12-month CD early can erase the entire advantage it was supposed to offer, sometimes down to literally nothing. If there's real uncertainty about when you'll need the money, that uncertainty itself is worth paying for — which, most of the time, means the liquid account wins by default.
