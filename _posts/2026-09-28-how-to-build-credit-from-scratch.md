I [broke down what actually moves a credit score]({{ '/what-actually-moves-your-credit-score/' | relative_url }}) once you have one, but skipped the more basic problem underneath it: what do you actually do when you have *no* credit history at all? Lenders want to see a track record before extending credit, but you can't build a track record without first getting some form of credit — it's a real chicken-and-egg problem, and it has a real, specific answer.

## The one-sentence version

You break into the credit system with a small, low-risk credit product specifically designed for people with no history — most commonly a secured credit card or becoming an authorized user on someone else's card — then use it lightly and pay it off in full every month until you qualify for better options.

<!--more-->

## Why "just get a credit card" doesn't work at zero

Most regular (unsecured) credit cards require some credit history to get approved in the first place — the bank is deciding whether to lend you money based on a track record, and with no track record, there's nothing to evaluate. This is the actual trap "credit invisible" describes: not bad credit, but no credit file at all, which can be just as much of an obstacle as bad credit when you're trying to rent an apartment or finance a car.

## The realistic starting points

**A secured credit card.** You put down a cash deposit (often $200-$500) that becomes your credit limit, and you use the card like a normal credit card from there — the deposit is the bank's protection against you not paying, which is exactly why they'll approve someone with zero history for one. Used responsibly, it reports to the credit bureaus like any other card, and many issuers will eventually refund the deposit and convert it to a regular unsecured card once you've built enough history.

**Becoming an authorized user.** If a parent or someone else you trust has a credit card with a long, positive history, they can add you as an authorized user — you get a card in your name, and their account's history (including its age, which matters for the "length of credit history" factor) can start showing up on your credit report too. This only helps if the primary cardholder actually manages the account well; an authorized user spot on a card with missed payments or high balances can hurt you the same way it would help.

**A credit-builder loan.** Offered by some banks and credit unions, this flips a normal loan backwards: instead of receiving money upfront and paying it back, you make payments into a locked account first, and get the money (plus, sometimes, a bit of interest) at the end. The payments themselves are what get reported and build your history — it's a loan built specifically to generate a track record, not to fund a purchase.

**A student credit card**, if you're enrolled in school — a category with more lenient approval criteria than a standard card, aimed specifically at people starting with no history.

## What to actually do once you have one

The product you start with matters less than what you do with it. The habits that build a strong score are the same regardless of which starting option you pick:

- **Charge something small and predictable** — a recurring subscription or a regular gas fill-up, not a purchase you couldn't otherwise afford.
- **Pay the statement balance in full, every month**, before the due date. This is the one habit that matters most: it builds positive payment history (the single biggest factor in your score) while costing you nothing in interest.
- **Don't chase a higher limit or use most of it.** Keeping your utilization low from the start is easier than fixing high utilization later.
- **Give it time.** Credit history has an actual "length of history" component — there's no way to shortcut months and years into existing, which is exactly why starting earlier (even with a small, boring secured card) beats waiting for a "better" credit product to become available to you.

## What not to do while starting out

Don't open several credit products at once trying to speed things up — each application triggers a hard inquiry, and multiple new accounts in a short window can actually work against a thin credit file rather than for it. Don't carry a balance thinking it "shows the bank you use credit" — as covered in the [credit score breakdown]({{ '/what-actually-moves-your-credit-score/' | relative_url }}), paying in full every month builds the same utilization benefit without paying interest for nothing. And don't skip building credit entirely because it feels unnecessary right now — a thin or nonexistent credit file becomes a real obstacle the moment you need it for something bigger, like a car loan or an apartment, at which point you're starting from zero exactly when you have the least patience to wait months for a score to develop.

## The takeaway

Building credit from nothing isn't complicated, it's just slow, and the products designed for exactly this situation — secured cards, authorized-user status, credit-builder loans — exist precisely because "no history" isn't the same problem as "bad history." Pick one, use it lightly, pay it off every month, and let time do the rest of the work; by the time you need a real credit history for something that matters, you'll already have one.
