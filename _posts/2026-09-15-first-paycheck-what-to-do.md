A first "real" paycheck — the kind with actual room to do something beyond covering bills — dumps a lot of decisions on you at once: save it, invest it, pay off debt, build a cushion, don't forget the employer match. I've written about most of these individually. Here's the order I'd actually put them in, and why the order matters as much as the individual pieces.

## Why order matters here

Each of these things I'm about to list is good on its own. But doing them in the wrong order costs you real money — maxing out investments before you have any emergency cushion means a car repair forces you to sell investments at a random, possibly bad, moment. The sequence below is built so each step protects the one before it.

<!--more-->

## The order

**1. Get the free money first: your employer's 401(k) match, if you have one.**
If your job offers a 401(k) match, contribute at least enough to get the full match before doing anything else on this list — turning down an instant 50-100% return has no equivalent anywhere else in personal finance. I wrote about [exactly how this works]({{ '/401k-employer-match/' | relative_url }}) if you haven't checked your specific match formula yet.

**2. Build a starter emergency fund — even a small one.**
Before anything else, get a few hundred to a thousand dollars into a separate, boring, liquid account. This isn't your full [emergency fund target]({{ '/emergency-fund-how-much/' | relative_url }}) yet — it's just enough that a flat tire or a broken phone doesn't become a credit card balance.

**3. Pay off high-interest debt.**
If you're carrying credit card debt at 20%+ interest, this comes before investing — paying it off is a guaranteed return no investment reliably beats. Lower-interest debt (federal student loans, an auto loan) is more of a judgment call and doesn't need to block the steps below.

**4. Finish the emergency fund.**
With high-interest debt handled, build the rest of the real emergency fund — the [3-12 months of essential expenses]({{ '/emergency-fund-how-much/' | relative_url }}) sized to your actual situation, not a flat number.

**5. Take advantage of tax-advantaged accounts.**
Once the fund is solid: a Roth IRA if you're eligible and expect to be in a higher bracket later, and an HSA if you have access to one through a high-deductible health plan — I've written about both: [Roth IRA]({{ '/what-is-a-roth-ira/' | relative_url }}), [HSA]({{ '/what-is-an-hsa/' | relative_url }}). The HSA in particular is easy to skip because it's filed under "health insurance," not "investing" — worth not making that mistake.

**6. Invest consistently, and don't overthink the account type beyond step 5.**
From here it's mostly about consistency: automate a recurring contribution into a [broad index fund]({{ '/index-funds-vs-individual-stocks/' | relative_url }}) — you're already [dollar-cost averaging]({{ '/dollar-cost-averaging/' | relative_url }}) by definition if it's coming out of every paycheck. This is the step people want to obsess over and it's actually the most mechanical one once the earlier steps are in place.

## What this looks like with an actual budget

If you're using something like the [50/30/20 framework]({{ '/50-30-20-budget-rule/' | relative_url }}) to figure out how much you even have to work with, steps 1-6 above are what the "20% savings" bucket is actually made of, in priority order — the framework tells you the size of the bucket, this list tells you what goes in it first.

## The honest caveat

This order is a reasonable default, not a rule carved in stone. Someone with genuinely unstable income might reasonably build a bigger emergency cushion before step 1; someone with employer stock or other specific benefits might have a step that doesn't fit neatly into this list at all. Treat this as the sequence to start from, and adjust for whatever about your specific situation makes a strict reading of it wrong.

## The takeaway

None of the individual pieces here are complicated — I've written a full explanation of almost every step already. The part that actually trips people up is doing them out of order: investing before there's a safety net, or building a giant emergency fund while turning down free employer match money the whole time. Get the sequence right and the rest is just consistency.
