I worked through [renting vs. buying]({{ '/renting-vs-buying-a-home/' | relative_url }}) assuming the home price in the comparison was already a reasonable one — I never actually explained how lenders, and most financial planning advice, land on what "reasonable" means. There's a specific, widely used rule for it, and the part that surprises people isn't the rule itself — it's how much an existing car payment or student loan can shrink the number.

## The one-sentence version

The 28/36 rule caps housing costs at 28% of gross monthly income and total debt (including housing) at 36% — and the lower of the two numbers is the one that actually applies, which means existing debt can shrink your home-buying budget well below what your income alone would suggest.

<!--more-->

## The two numbers, worked out

On an $85,000 household income (**$7,083/month** gross), the two caps:

| | Cap | Monthly amount |
|---|---|---|
| Front-end (housing only) | 28% of gross income | **$1,983** |
| Back-end (all debt, including housing) | 36% of gross income | **$2,550** |

With no other debt, the front-end number is the one that matters — $1,983/month is comfortably under the $2,550 back-end cap, so it's the binding constraint. That changes the moment other debt enters the picture.

## The part that actually surprises people

Same $85,000 income, but now with a **$550/month car payment and a $300/month student loan payment** — $850/month in existing debt:

| | Cap | Monthly amount available |
|---|---|---|
| Back-end cap minus existing debt | 36% minus $850/mo already committed | **$1,700** |

**$1,700 is now the real housing budget — $283/month less than the front-end cap alone would have suggested**, because the back-end ratio became the binding constraint the moment other debt entered the picture. Translated into an approximate mortgage size (at a representative 6.5% rate, 30-year term, after setting aside roughly 15% of the housing payment for taxes and insurance): **roughly $267,000 of home with no other debt, versus roughly $229,000 with the car and student loan payments — about $38,000 less home, same income, same credit, same down payment.** The only thing that changed was debt that had nothing to do with the house itself.

## Why this catches people off guard

Two people earning the identical salary can qualify for meaningfully different mortgage amounts, and the difference has nothing to do with how "responsible" either of them is with money in the moment — it's a direct, mechanical consequence of existing monthly obligations eating into the same 36% ceiling the mortgage payment also has to fit inside. This is exactly why paying down a car loan or reducing other debt before a mortgage application can directly increase what you qualify for, independent of saving a larger down payment or raising your income.

## It's a guideline, not a hard wall

Worth being precise about what this rule actually is: a widely used underwriting guideline, not a universal legal cutoff. Lenders routinely approve borrowers above a 36% back-end ratio depending on credit history, the size of the down payment, cash reserves, and the specific loan program — some loan types are explicitly built to accommodate higher ratios for qualified borrowers. The 28/36 numbers are the right starting point for a realistic self-assessment, not necessarily the exact ceiling a specific lender will apply.

## The part everyone glosses over: this uses gross income, not take-home pay

Both ratios are calculated against **gross** (pre-tax) income, not the take-home pay that actually lands in a checking account — which makes the affordable-sounding number larger than what a budget actually has available in practice. Someone running this math should mentally translate the housing-budget figure against their real after-tax, after-401(k)-contribution take-home pay before treating it as a comfortable number, not just a qualifying one.

## Qualifying for it isn't the same as it being the right amount

Everything above answers "what would a lender likely approve," which is a different question from "what should I actually spend." A payment at the edge of the 36% ceiling leaves little room for [an emergency fund]({{ '/emergency-fund-how-much/' | relative_url }}), [extra mortgage payments or investing]({{ '/extra-mortgage-payments-or-invest/' | relative_url }}), or an unexpected expense — qualifying for the maximum and choosing to spend meaningfully under it is a completely reasonable, common decision, not a failure to use available credit.

## The takeaway

"How much house can I afford" has a concrete, checkable starting answer — 28% of gross income for housing, 36% for all debt combined, whichever is lower — and the number most people get wrong isn't the percentage, it's forgetting that existing debt directly competes with the mortgage for the same 36% ceiling. Check both numbers against your actual gross income and actual existing debt before assuming a home price is realistic, and treat whatever a lender approves as a ceiling to consider, not a target to hit.
