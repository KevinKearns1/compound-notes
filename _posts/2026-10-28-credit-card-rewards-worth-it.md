Credit card rewards get marketed like free money, and they can genuinely be close to that — but only under one specific condition that doesn't get stated often enough. The real math, worked through, shows how little it actually takes to erase an entire year of cashback.

## The one-sentence version

Credit card rewards (cashback or points) are real value if you pay your statement balance in full every month, and the [APR]({{ '/what-is-apr/' | relative_url }}) on any carried balance can wipe out far more value than the rewards ever earned — often from carrying a surprisingly small balance for a surprisingly short time.

<!--more-->

## The rewards side, in real numbers

A 2% cashback card on $24,000 of annual spending (about $2,000/month, a reasonable number for many households' regular expenses routed through one card) earns **$480 a year** — real money, for buying things you were already going to buy, at no cost beyond using the card instead of cash or a debit card.

## The cost side, in the same real numbers

Here's where it gets stark. At a representative 22% [APR]({{ '/what-is-apr/' | relative_url }}), carrying an **average balance of about $2,182 across the full year** costs exactly $480 in interest — the same amount the entire year of 2% cashback earned. That's not a large balance by credit card standards; it's well within what a single unexpected expense or one slow month of paying down a balance can produce.

Even smaller and shorter: carrying one average month's spending ($2,000) for a single billing cycle costs about **$37 in interest** — which is roughly 92% of that month's entire cashback earnings, wiped out by less than five weeks of carrying a balance that was going to get paid off anyway, just a cycle later than planned.

## Why this makes the rule simple, not complicated

The entire rewards-card value proposition collapses the moment a balance gets carried, because the [APR]({{ '/what-is-apr/' | relative_url }}) on a typical card (commonly 20%+) is roughly **ten times** a typical rewards rate (commonly 1-3%). There's no card and no rewards rate clever enough to outrun that gap through rewards alone — the entire strategy only works in the specific case where interest never applies, which means: **paying the statement balance in full, every single month, with no exceptions.** This is the same habit that, as covered in the [credit score post]({{ '/what-actually-moves-your-credit-score/' | relative_url }}), builds credit the same way carrying a balance does, with none of the interest cost.

## The other hidden cost: spending more to chase rewards

A real, well-documented behavioral effect: having a rewards card measurably increases how much people spend, compared to paying with cash or debit for the same purchases — a few extra percentage points of spending to "earn more rewards" easily outweighs the rewards rate itself. A 2% cashback card that encourages 5% more total spending isn't a 2% win, it's a net loss, and this effect is exactly why "I'll just use the rewards card for everything" deserves more scrutiny than it usually gets.

## Annual fees: when the math requires more spending than it looks like

Premium rewards cards often charge an annual fee ($95, $250, sometimes more) in exchange for a higher rewards rate. The breakeven math: upgrading from a 2% no-fee card to a 3% card with a $95 annual fee requires **$9,500 in annual spending** on that one extra percentage point just to cover the fee — meaningful spending that needs to actually happen on that specific card before the "better" card is actually better. Below that spending level, the no-fee card wins on pure math, regardless of how good the premium card's rewards rate looks on paper.

## When rewards are genuinely free money

All of this is a real, legitimate case for rewards cards, not an argument against them — for someone who already pays in full every month out of habit (the same discipline [the APR post]({{ '/what-is-apr/' | relative_url }}) argued for regardless of rewards), a cashback or points card earning a percentage back on money that was being spent anyway is close to the closest thing to free money that exists in personal finance. The entire question of "worth it" comes down to one habit, not the specific card, the rewards rate, or the sign-up bonus.

## The takeaway

Credit card rewards are real value under exactly one condition — paying in full every month — and the math shows how thin the margin is the moment that condition breaks: a carried balance well under $2,200 for a year, or even a single month's spending carried one cycle too long, can cost more in interest than an entire year of cashback ever earned. The right question isn't "which card has the best rewards," it's "am I someone who reliably pays in full" — the rewards rate is a rounding error compared to that answer.
