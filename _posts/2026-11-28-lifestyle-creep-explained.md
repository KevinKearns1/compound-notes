I wrote about [the math behind negotiating a raise]({{ '/negotiating-your-salary/' | relative_url }}) without ever covering the part that happens right after you get one: what you actually do with it. Spending the whole thing and banking half of it feel almost identical in the moment — a little more room in the budget either way — and that's exactly why the gap between them is so easy to miss until years later, when it's much too large to shrug off.

## The one-sentence version

Lifestyle creep is spending rising to match every raise instead of falling behind it, and because it happens one small, reasonable-feeling upgrade at a time, it quietly erases most of the benefit of earning more — even though the paycheck itself never stopped growing.

<!--more-->

## The actual math

Two people, same starting salary, same raises, same 10 years. Both start by saving 15% of a $60,000 salary — $9,000 a year — into an account earning 7% annually. The only difference: what happens to each year's 3% raise.

**Saver A** spends every raise. Their dollar savings amount never changes — still exactly $9,000 a year, every year, for 10 years.

**Saver B** banks half of each year's raise into savings and spends the other half. Their savings amount grows a little every year, but far more slowly than their salary does.

| | Saver A (spends every raise) | Saver B (banks half of each raise) |
|---|---|---|
| Savings rate, year 1 | 15.0% | 15.0% |
| Savings rate, year 10 | **11.5%** | **23.2%** |
| Total contributed over 10 years | $90,000 | $133,916 |
| Balance after 10 years (7% return) | $133,052 | **$189,690** |
| Spendable income, year 10 | $69,286 | $60,143 |

Saver B ends up with **$56,638 more** after 10 years. Saver A gets to spend about $9,100 more per year by year 10 to get there. Neither of those numbers is a trick — they're the same trade-off, just measured from two different directions.

## Why "I'm still saving $9,000 a year" feels fine and isn't

This is the part that makes lifestyle creep so easy to miss: Saver A never stopped saving. They're still putting away the exact same $9,000 every year they always have — nothing about their behavior looks like backsliding. But their salary grew 30% over those 10 years while their savings amount grew 0%, so their **savings rate** quietly fell from 15% to 11.5% without a single decision that felt like a cut. That's the mechanism: lifestyle creep doesn't require overspending in any one year, just holding the dollar amount saved constant while the number it's a percentage of keeps climbing.

## Why this connects to compound interest

[Compound interest]({{ '/what-is-compound-interest/' | relative_url }}) rewards money that goes in earlier and stays in longer. Every dollar of a raise spent instead of saved isn't just that one dollar — it's that dollar's entire remaining runway to compound, gone. Saver B's advantage in the table above isn't mostly from the extra $43,916 they contributed; a meaningful share of the $56,638 gap is the compounding those contributions did after arriving earlier in the 10-year window than an equivalent lump sum would have arriving at the end.

## This isn't an argument for banking every raise

Saver A isn't making a mistake, exactly — spending 100% of a raise on a real upgrade in quality of life (a safer car, a better apartment, actually taking a vacation) is a legitimate choice, not a failure of discipline. The point isn't that Saver A is wrong and Saver B is right. It's that **this is a real, quantifiable decision being made, whether or not it's made on purpose** — and most lifestyle creep happens by default, one plausible-sounding upgrade at a time, never as a single conscious choice to keep the savings rate flat for a decade.

## The practical version

The easiest version of Saver B's approach isn't "live like nothing changed" — it's picking a fraction of every future raise, before it arrives, to route straight into savings or investing, and letting the rest flow into spending guilt-free. Half is a clean, workable default; even a smaller fraction beats the 0% that a frozen dollar-amount savings habit defaults to without anyone deciding it should. This is the same idea behind the savings bucket in the [50/30/20 budget]({{ '/50-30-20-budget-rule/' | relative_url }}) — the trick is making sure that bucket's *dollar amount* actually moves when income does, not just its percentage on paper.

## The takeaway

Lifestyle creep isn't spending too much in any single year — it's a savings rate that quietly declines while the dollar amount saved stays frozen and salary keeps climbing around it. Banking even half of every future raise, as a standing rule instead of a yearly decision, is the difference between a savings rate that erodes by default and one that grows the same way the paycheck does.
