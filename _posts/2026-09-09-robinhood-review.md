## What it actually is

Robinhood is a mobile-first brokerage — I can open an individual brokerage account, a retirement account, and trade stocks, ETFs, options, and crypto, all from an app built to feel more like a consumer app than a trading terminal. That design choice is the whole story of both what it does well and where it falls short.

<!--more-->

## What it gets right

**No account minimum.** I could open an account and start with whatever amount I was comfortable with — there's no "you need $500 to begin" barrier, which matters a lot for the exact audience I'm writing this site for (people investing $20-50 a month, not people with a lump sum to deploy).

**Fractional shares.** You don't need a full share's worth of money to own a piece of an expensive stock — you can buy a fraction of a share for whatever dollar amount you choose. For a beginner without much capital, this is a genuinely bigger deal than it sounds; it's the difference between "I can only afford stocks under $50" and "I can invest in anything with $10."

**The interface is legitimately easy to use.** Whatever else is true, the app doesn't require you to already understand trading terminology to place a basic order. That's a real, deliberate design choice, and it's a big part of why I recommend it to people starting out.

**Commission-free trades and no account fees.** This used to be Robinhood's headline differentiator; it's now closer to industry standard since most major brokerages followed suit, but it's still true and still worth confirming versus whichever alternative you're comparing it to.

**A real referral program.** Both sides get something — I get $50, you get somewhere between $5 and $200 in stock or cash. Robinhood sets the exact terms and they can change, so I'd rather point you to the current details than promise you a specific number.

## Where it genuinely falls short

**Its research and analysis tools are thin.** If you want deep fundamental data, analyst reports, or advanced charting built into the platform, Robinhood isn't where you'll find it — you'll likely end up looking things up elsewhere and executing the trade in the app. For simple, long-term index-fund investing, this barely matters to me. If you want to research individual stocks seriously, it's a real limitation.

**It's had real reliability problems during high-volatility events.** Robinhood's platform has had outages, and during the January 2021 meme-stock volatility it controversially restricted buying on certain stocks — a decision that drew serious criticism and regulatory scrutiny. That's a real mark against it, not a hypothetical concern, even though I haven't had day-to-day reliability issues since.

**Payment for order flow.** Like most commission-free brokerages, Robinhood makes money in part by routing trade orders through market makers who pay for that order flow, rather than charging a commission directly. Regulators and researchers have studied whether this costs retail investors in execution quality; the evidence is mixed and this isn't unique to Robinhood, but it's worth understanding rather than assuming "commission-free" means "no cost anywhere in the chain."

**It's app-first, sometimes to a fault.** If you want a full-featured desktop trading platform, Robinhood's web experience has historically been a secondary experience to the mobile app, not the other way around.

## Who I think it's actually a good fit for

Based on all of the above, I think Robinhood makes the most sense if you're a **beginner doing straightforward, long-term investing with amounts that don't require deep research tools** — which is most of who I'm writing this site for. It makes less sense if you want to actively trade with serious charting and research needs, or if the payment-for-order-flow model genuinely bothers you and you'd rather use a brokerage that doesn't rely on it.
