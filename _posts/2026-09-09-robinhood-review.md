Compound Notes mentions Robinhood a lot, because it's the brokerage the writer actually uses — but "I use it" isn't the same as "here's an honest assessment of it," and this site's [disclosure]({{ '/disclosure/' | relative_url }}) says real downsides get said out loud, referral link or not. So here's the actual review, pros and cons, instead of just another mention in passing.

## What it actually is

Robinhood is a mobile-first brokerage — you can open an individual brokerage account, a retirement account, and trade stocks, ETFs, options, and crypto, all from an app built to feel more like a consumer app than a trading terminal. That design choice is the whole story of both what it does well and where it falls short.

<!--more-->

## What it gets right

**No account minimum.** You can open an account and start with whatever amount you're comfortable with — there's no "you need $500 to begin" barrier, which matters a lot for the exact audience this site is written for (people investing $20-50 a month, not people with a lump sum to deploy).

**Fractional shares.** You don't need a full share's worth of money to own a piece of an expensive stock — you can buy a fraction of a share for whatever dollar amount you choose. For a beginner without much capital, this is a genuinely bigger deal than it sounds; it's the difference between "I can only afford stocks under $50" and "I can invest in anything with $10."

**The interface is legitimately easy to use.** Whatever else is true, the app does not require you to already understand trading terminology to place a basic order. That's a real, deliberate design choice, and it lowers the barrier to actually starting versus a brokerage built around a dense professional-trader interface.

**Commission-free trades and no account fees.** This used to be Robinhood's headline differentiator; it's now closer to industry standard since most major brokerages followed suit, but it's still true and still worth confirming versus whichever alternative you're comparing it to.

**A real referral program.** If you sign up through a referral link (including the one on this site's [Affiliate Links page]({{ '/affiliate-links/' | relative_url }})), both the referrer and the new account may receive a stock reward — Robinhood sets the exact terms, and they change, so check them directly before counting on a specific number.

## Where it genuinely falls short

**Its research and analysis tools are thin.** If you want deep fundamental data, analyst reports, or advanced charting built into the platform, Robinhood is not where you'll find it — you'll likely end up looking things up elsewhere and executing the trade in the app. For a beginner doing simple, long-term index-fund investing, this barely matters. For anyone wanting to research individual stocks seriously, it's a real limitation.

**It's had real reliability problems during high-volatility events.** Robinhood's platform has experienced outages and, during the January 2021 meme-stock volatility, controversially restricted buying on certain stocks — a decision that drew serious criticism and regulatory scrutiny. That's a real historical mark against it, not a hypothetical concern, and worth knowing about even though day-to-day reliability has generally been fine since.

**Payment for order flow.** Like most commission-free brokerages, Robinhood makes money in part by routing your trade orders through market makers who pay for that order flow, rather than charging you a commission directly. Regulators and researchers have studied whether this costs retail investors in execution quality; the evidence is mixed and this practice isn't unique to Robinhood, but it's a real thing worth understanding rather than assuming "commission-free" means "no cost to you anywhere in the chain."

**It's app-first, sometimes to a fault.** If you want a full-featured desktop trading platform, Robinhood's web experience has historically been a secondary experience to the mobile app, not the other way around.

## Who it's actually a good fit for

Based on the above, Robinhood makes the most sense for **beginners doing straightforward, long-term investing with amounts that don't require deep research tools** — which, not coincidentally, describes most of the audience this site is written for. It makes less sense for someone who wants to actively trade with serious charting and research needs, or who's specifically bothered by the payment-for-order-flow model and wants a brokerage that doesn't use it.
