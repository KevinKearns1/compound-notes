I covered [HELOCs and home equity loans]({{ '/heloc-vs-home-equity-loan/' | relative_url }}) as two ways to borrow against a home's equity without covering the third, more common option — and it has a specific cost trap neither of the other two does.

## The one-sentence version

A cash-out refinance doesn't add a new loan on top of the existing mortgage the way a HELOC or home equity loan does — it **replaces the entire mortgage** with a new, larger one at today's rate, which means anyone who locked in a lower rate in recent years isn't just borrowing new money at today's rate, they're resetting the rate on their *entire original balance* too.

<!--more-->

## What it actually is

A cash-out refinance pays off the existing mortgage completely and replaces it with a brand-new, larger loan — the difference between the new loan amount and what was owed on the old one comes back as a lump sum of cash. It's a full mortgage transaction, with the closing costs (origination, appraisal, title work) that come with one, not the lighter closing costs typical of a HELOC or home equity loan sitting as a separate lien on top of an unchanged first mortgage.

## The trap, worked out in real numbers

Here's a homeowner with a **$300,000 mortgage at 3.5%** (a realistic rate for anyone who bought or refinanced in 2020-2021), who wants to pull out **$50,000** in cash — compared two ways, using a representative current refinance rate of 6.61% and a representative current HELOC rate of 7.18%:

| | Cash-out refinance | Keep the mortgage + add a HELOC |
|---|---|---|
| What gets the new rate | **The entire $350,000** (old balance + cash out) | **Only the new $50,000** |
| Monthly cost | **$2,238** | **$1,646** ($1,347 old mortgage + $299 HELOC interest-only) |

**The refinance costs about $591 more per month — roughly $7,100 per year** — not because the cash-out amount is different, but because the refinance resets the interest rate on the *entire original $300,000*, not just the $50,000 actually being borrowed. The HELOC leaves that original low rate completely untouched and only prices the new money at today's rate.

## Why this is easy to miss

The instinct is to compare "what rate am I getting" against "what rate would a HELOC charge," as if the comparison were only about the $50,000 being pulled out. It isn't. A cash-out refinance pays off and replaces the *whole* loan — there's no mechanism that lets the original balance keep its old rate while only the new portion gets the new one. Anyone sitting on a mortgage rate noticeably below where current rates stand is, often without realizing it, holding one of the more valuable things in their entire financial picture: a large loan balance at a below-market rate. A cash-out refinance gives that up entirely, on the full balance, not just the portion being added.

## When a cash-out refinance still makes sense

**When current rates are close to or below the existing mortgage rate.** If there's no meaningful rate to lose, the "resetting the whole balance" cost mostly disappears, and a refinance's single-payment simplicity becomes a real advantage over juggling two separate loans.

**When one simple, fixed payment matters more than minimizing cost.** A cash-out refinance produces one loan, one fixed rate, one monthly payment — genuinely simpler than tracking a mortgage and a HELOC with its own variable rate and its own draw-to-repayment transition.

**When the amount needed is large relative to the existing balance.** The math above is most dramatic for someone cashing out a relatively small amount against a large, low-rate existing balance. Someone needing to borrow an amount closer to the size of their current balance anyway has less of an existing low rate left to protect.

## The takeaway

A cash-out refinance and a HELOC can produce wildly different costs for pulling out the identical amount of cash, and the difference has nothing to do with which lender offers a better rate — it's a structural fact about what each product actually resets. Before assuming a refinance is the obvious move, it's worth checking one specific thing: what rate is currently on the existing mortgage, and what would current rates do to the entire balance, not just the new money being borrowed.
