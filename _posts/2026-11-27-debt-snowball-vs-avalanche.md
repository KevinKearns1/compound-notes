I've written about [paying off student loans vs. investing]({{ '/pay-off-student-loans-or-invest/' | relative_url }}) and about specific debt traps like [balance transfers]({{ '/balance-transfer-credit-cards/' | relative_url }}), but never the actual question that comes up the moment someone has more than one debt at the same time: which one do you pay off first? There are two competing answers, and the honest one is that they're both right, for different reasons.

## The one-sentence version

The debt avalanche (highest interest rate first) mathematically minimizes the total interest paid; the debt snowball (smallest balance first) pays a little more in interest but produces faster "paid off" wins that research suggests actually help more people finish — so the "best" method depends on which one you'll actually stick with.

<!--more-->

## How each method actually works

Both methods start the same way: keep making the minimum payment on every debt, and throw every extra dollar you can find at just one of them. They only disagree on *which one*.

**Debt avalanche:** send the extra money to whichever debt has the **highest interest rate**, regardless of balance. Once that one's gone, roll its old payment into the next-highest rate, and so on.

**Debt snowball:** send the extra money to whichever debt has the **smallest balance**, regardless of rate. Once that one's gone, roll its old payment into the next-smallest balance, and so on.

## The math, worked through

Here are three debts, sized and rated so the two methods genuinely disagree about where to start — a small loan with a low rate, a mid-size card with a high rate, and a large loan in between:

| Debt | Balance | APR | Minimum payment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Student loan | $1,500 | 5% | $50 |
| Credit card | $4,000 | 24% | $120 |
| Personal loan | $10,000 | 13% | $220 |

Minimums total $390/month. Add $250/month extra, for a fixed $640/month going toward debt either way — only the *order* changes between the two methods. Avalanche attacks the credit card first (highest rate, 24%); snowball attacks the student loan first (smallest balance, $1,500), leaving the 24% card accruing interest longer.

| | Debt avalanche | Debt snowball |
|---|---|---|
| Order of attack | Card (24%) → Personal loan (13%) → Student loan (5%) | Student loan ($1,500) → Card ($4,000) → Personal loan ($10,000) |
| First debt fully gone | Month 13 | **Month 6** |
| Total interest paid | **$2,680.72** | $3,009.38 |
| Total months to debt-free | 29 | 29 |

Avalanche saves **$328.66** in interest here, for the same 29 months to being completely debt-free — the total payoff time barely moves, because the fixed monthly budget and total principal are the same either way. What actually differs is where the interest goes and how fast the first win shows up.

## Why the "worse" method still has a real case for it

$328.66 sounds like an easy call in avalanche's favor, until you look at the other number in that table: snowball produces a fully eliminated debt by **month 6**, more than twice as fast as avalanche's **month 13**. That's not a minor detail — a 2012 Kellogg School study of real credit-card repayment data found that people carrying multiple balances were more likely to pay off their debt *entirely* when they tackled the smallest balances first, even though it isn't the mathematically optimal order, and a later 2016 study of roughly 6,000 debtors over 36 months found the snowball approach tends to help most specifically when the various debts' interest rates aren't wildly different from each other — which is exactly the condition where the dollar cost of "doing it wrong" is smallest anyway.

The honest read: avalanche is the better plan on paper for someone who will follow a 29-month plan regardless of how it feels along the way. Snowball is a deliberate trade — a modest, quantifiable amount of extra interest, in exchange for the first visible proof the plan is working showing up twice as fast, which is worth something real if that's the difference between finishing and quietly giving up around month 10.

## A middle option worth knowing

Nothing requires picking one pure method. A common hybrid: start with the smallest balance for the early motivational win, then switch to attacking the highest remaining rate for the rest of the payoff. It gives up a little of each method's advantage but captures most of both — a reasonable default for anyone who isn't sure which failure mode (losing motivation vs. leaving money on the table) they're more at risk of.

## What doesn't change either way

Whichever order you pick, the fundamentals from [what to do with your first paycheck]({{ '/first-paycheck-what-to-do/' | relative_url }}) still apply underneath it: keep making every minimum payment on time regardless of strategy (a missed minimum hurts your credit and can trigger penalty rates no payoff order fixes), and don't let an all-in debt attack leave you with zero [emergency fund]({{ '/emergency-fund-how-much/' | relative_url }}) — an unplanned expense with nothing saved just becomes a new debt, undoing whatever order you picked.

## The takeaway

Debt avalanche and debt snowball aren't a math-vs-nonsense argument — avalanche genuinely saves more money, and snowball genuinely helps more people actually finish, according to real repayment data, not just motivational folklore. The $328.66 difference in the example above is real, but so is the gap between a plan you stick with for 29 months and one you abandon at month 10. Pick based on which failure you're actually more likely to have: running the numbers wrong, or running out of patience.
