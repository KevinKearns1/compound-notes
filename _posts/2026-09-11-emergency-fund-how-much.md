I mentioned emergency funds in passing when I wrote about [saving vs. investing]({{ '/saving-vs-investing/' | relative_url }}), but I never actually answered the question everyone asks next: how much is enough? The honest answer is "it depends," but that's a useless sentence on its own — here's what it actually depends on.

## The two answers you'll usually hear, and why neither is complete

**"$1,000"** — this number floats around as a starter goal, and as a starter goal it's fine. But it's not sized to your life, it's sized to be a round number that's better than nothing. Treating $1,000 as the finish line instead of the starting line is where this advice goes wrong.

**"3 to 6 months of expenses"** — closer to right, but it's a range with no instructions for where you personally fall in it, and "expenses" is doing a lot of unexamined work in that sentence.

<!--more-->

## What actually moves you toward 3 months vs. 6 (or more)

**How stable is your income?** A salaried job at a large, stable employer and 1099 freelance income with unpredictable monthly swings are not the same risk. The less predictable your income, the more months you want banked — some people with genuinely volatile income reasonably keep 9-12 months.

**How replaceable is your specific job?** A role with high demand and lots of similar openings gets you back to income faster after a job loss than a narrow specialty with few employers. Faster replacement time supports a smaller fund; longer expected job-search time supports a bigger one.

**Do you have other people depending on your income?** A single person with no dependents has more room to cut expenses fast in a real emergency than someone supporting a family, where costs don't shrink as easily on short notice.

**Do you have a second income in the household?** Two incomes covering one household's expenses is a real cushion by itself — if one income stops, the other is still covering something. A single-income household carrying the whole cost of living has no equivalent backstop, which argues for a bigger fund.

**How much of your "expenses" are actually fixed?** This is the part "6 months of expenses" usually glosses over. If a chunk of your spending is genuinely discretionary (subscriptions, dining out, upgrades you could pause instantly), your true bare-bones survival number is lower than your full monthly spending — and it's your bare-bones number that should set the fund size, not your comfortable-lifestyle number.

## A more useful way to actually calculate it

1. Add up your genuinely essential monthly costs: housing, utilities, minimum debt payments, groceries, insurance, transportation. Not your full budget — your floor.
2. Multiply that floor by a number between 3 and 12 based on the factors above — more stable income and a replaceable job pulls you toward 3; unpredictable income, a narrow specialty, dependents, or a single income pulls you toward 6-12.
3. That's your target. Not $1,000, not a generic "6 months" — a number that's actually about your situation.

## Where to actually keep it

The fund only works if it's there, intact, the moment you need it — which rules out anywhere the value can drop. That means **not** in stocks or index funds, no matter how good the expected long-term return looks; a real emergency has no obligation to wait for a bad market month to pass before it happens.

A **high-yield savings account** is the standard answer, and it's standard for good reason: FDIC-insured (protected up to the legal limit if the bank fails), the balance never drops from market movement, and it's liquid — accessible within a day or two, not locked up. The "high-yield" part matters too: the interest rate difference between a big traditional bank's near-zero savings rate and an online high-yield account can be significant, and there's no tradeoff for taking it — same safety, same liquidity, meaningfully more interest.

One nuance worth knowing: keeping it at a **different bank than your everyday checking account** is a deliberate choice some people make on purpose — it adds just enough friction (a transfer that takes a day, rather than an instant move) to stop the fund from quietly becoming a backup shopping budget, while still being fully accessible in an actual emergency.

## The takeaway

Skip the flat number and the vague range. Add up your real floor, adjust the multiplier for how stable your specific situation actually is, and keep the result somewhere boring, insured, and liquid — not invested, not comfortable to raid, just there.
