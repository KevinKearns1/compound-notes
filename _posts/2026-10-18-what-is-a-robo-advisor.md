I've described two hands-off approaches so far — a [target-date fund]({{ '/what-is-a-target-date-fund/' | relative_url }}) for retirement accounts, and a DIY two-fund mix you [rebalance]({{ '/what-is-rebalancing/' | relative_url }}) yourself. There's a third option that gets marketed heavily: the robo-advisor. Here's what it's actually doing, since "an algorithm manages your money" undersells it and oversells it at the same time.

## The one-sentence version

A robo-advisor is an automated investing service that builds you a [diversified]({{ '/what-is-diversification/' | relative_url }}) portfolio of [index funds]({{ '/etf-vs-mutual-fund-vs-index-fund/' | relative_url }}) based on your answers to a short questionnaire, then automatically invests new contributions and [rebalances]({{ '/what-is-rebalancing/' | relative_url }}) the portfolio over time — for a fee, typically a small percentage of your balance per year.

<!--more-->

## What actually happens when you sign up

You answer a handful of questions — your age, goals, income, and how you'd react to the portfolio dropping 20% in a year (the actual purpose of these risk-tolerance questions). Based on your answers, the service assigns you a stock-to-bond mix, similar in spirit to the [age-based allocation]({{ '/stocks-vs-bonds-by-age/' | relative_url }}) logic covered earlier, then builds your account using a set of low-cost index funds to hit that target. From there, new deposits get automatically invested according to that mix, and the whole portfolio periodically rebalances back to target without you doing anything.

This is, mechanically, the same underlying idea as a target-date fund — automated allocation and rebalancing — but applied more broadly: it works across a whole account (not just one retirement fund), can account for multiple goals, and in a taxable account often adds **tax-loss harvesting** (automatically selling a losing position to offset gains elsewhere, a real technique touched on in the [capital gains post]({{ '/capital-gains-tax-explained/' | relative_url }})) as part of the service.

## What the fee actually buys you

Robo-advisors typically charge somewhere in the range of **0.25%-0.50% of your balance per year**, on top of the underlying funds' own [expense ratios]({{ '/what-is-an-expense-ratio/' | relative_url }}). That fee is paying for three things: the initial allocation decision, ongoing automatic rebalancing, and (in taxable accounts) automated tax-loss harvesting — none of which are things you couldn't do yourself with a bit of time and a two-fund DIY approach, which is the actual tradeoff worth understanding.

## The honest comparison: robo-advisor vs. doing it yourself

| | DIY two-fund portfolio | Robo-advisor |
|---|---|---|
| Ongoing fee | Just the underlying funds' expense ratio (often under 0.10%) | Expense ratio + 0.25%-0.50% advisory fee |
| Rebalancing | You do it, on a schedule or threshold | Automatic |
| Tax-loss harvesting | You'd have to do this manually, if at all | Often automated |
| Effort required | Periodic check-ins | Essentially none |

Over decades, that extra 0.25%-0.50% fee compounds the same way [any fee does]({{ '/what-is-an-expense-ratio/' | relative_url }}) — it's a real, ongoing cost, not a one-time charge. Whether it's worth paying comes down to one honest question: would you actually rebalance a DIY portfolio consistently, or would it drift the way an unmanaged portfolio tends to? If the honest answer is "probably not," the fee is buying real discipline you weren't going to supply yourself — the same logic that makes a target-date fund's fee reasonable for the same kind of person.

## What a robo-advisor isn't

It's not a magic algorithm picking winning stocks — underneath, it's typically the same broad index funds you could buy yourself, just automatically allocated and maintained. It's not free, even when the marketing emphasizes "no commissions" — the advisory fee is the actual cost structure. And it's not a substitute for financial planning in a complicated situation — it handles portfolio construction and maintenance well, but a genuinely complex situation (business ownership, estate planning, multiple competing goals) usually still benefits from a human who can ask questions a questionnaire can't.

## When it's genuinely the right call

- **You know you won't rebalance a DIY portfolio consistently**, and the fee is a reasonable price for solving that specific, real problem.
- **You want tax-loss harvesting in a taxable account** but don't want to track and execute it yourself.
- **You want one place that handles multiple goals** (a house fund, retirement, a general investing account) with different allocations, without managing several accounts by hand.

## When the DIY route makes more sense

- **You're disciplined enough to rebalance on a schedule** — the fee is then paying mostly for convenience you don't strictly need.
- **Your account is entirely tax-advantaged** (inside an [IRA or 401(k)]({{ '/traditional-vs-roth-ira/' | relative_url }})) — tax-loss harvesting doesn't apply there, so a chunk of the robo-advisor's value proposition disappears.
- **You're comfortable with a simple two- or three-fund portfolio** and checking in on it once or twice a year.

## The takeaway

A robo-advisor isn't a different investment strategy than the broad-index-fund approach covered throughout this blog — it's the same strategy, automated, for a fee. That fee buys real convenience and discipline for people who'd otherwise let a portfolio drift, and it's a legitimately reasonable trade for that person. For someone willing to rebalance on their own schedule, the same outcome is available for a fraction of the cost.
